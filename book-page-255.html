Exhibition matches

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 255 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

Tauranga has long sort ways to bring top golf to the district and though the likes of Gene Sarazen and Bobby Locke, who each toured the country in their prime, could not be attracted to the club other options were pursued. In 1960 Kel Nagle played Jim Paterson, the match finishing all square with Nagle scoring 68, Paterson 69. For their pains Nagle was paid £75, Paterson £25, while the club lost £25 on the day and felt it was money well spent.

Another popular Australian, Peter Thomson, participated in an exhibition match with Fred Ward when that fine player was at the peak of form. Some who were present recall Thomson hooking his drive under the hedge that ran the entire length of the boundary to the left of the fifteenth fairway. This Lawsoniana hedge was wide and the branches reached the ground. Anyone who attempted to play from inside the hedge, as Thomson did, was virtually invisible. The ball emerged followed by the player who quickly advised his opponent and the gallery that he had played an “air shot” when his club struck branches on his downswing. That would have been a rare event in the life of one of the greatest of British Open Champions.

In the year following the Nagle exhibition Bob Charles played a match against Jimmy Paterson. Once again the local Professional gave a good account of himself against outstanding opposition. On this occasion Charles was the winner by two and one following an excellent display by both men before a large and appreciative gallery.

The fifteenth hole has been the subject of some interesting observations. Though little longer than the sixteenth it always seems to play much longer. It is well within the recognised length for a par four for men and just above the upper limit for women. It is rated par five on the women’s card. The competitors in the top 16 in the Una Wickham Memorial Tournament

played off the blue tees while the remainder played off the yellow. For many years during that tournament the yellow tees were placed on the back of the fifteenth tee, the blues on the front. This happened on no other tee. When asked why this was done the official response was that the hole was played as a par four for the top group, a par five for the rest. When asked what importance this had in a tournament that was entirely Match Play the enquirer met only with a perplexed look. It was many years before it was realised that the top players should be offered the greatest challenge at every hole.

It was even more perplexing to see the reigning New Zealand Women’s Stroke Play Champion arrive at the fifteenth tee to find no yellow blocks. This was during the Carrus Tournament when the women were playing from yellow, the men from blue tees. It soon transpired that the blocks were about fifty metres ahead in the fairway where they had been placed by the Tournament Director who was an outside ‘expert’ supposedly well steeped in golfing lore though perhaps not in common sense! We can only presume the young star needed the head start in order to have a chance to play the hole in four strokes to match the men she was no longer competing against.

To add to the quandary this young lady was the only woman to survive the 36-hole cut thus displacing a male player who had played off quite different tees.
