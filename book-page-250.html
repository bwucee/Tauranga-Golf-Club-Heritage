The final day developed into a contest between Wolstenholme and Owen, though not head to head as Owen was two or three holes ahead in the field. Owen made some ground over the early holes before Wolstenholme regained his four-stroke lead with an eagle on the 7th. When Owen hit out of bounds at the 10th and then compounded the error by three putting the green his challenge was seemingly over.

To his credit he did not allow the mistake to ruin his round, scoring three birdies in the final eight holes to post a 67. As Owen walked the 18th fairway he was still four behind Wolstenholme who was then playing the 16th. When they completed those holes the margin was down to a single stroke, Owen holing a four metre birdie putt while his rival struck tree trouble, then three putted for a double bogey six. Incredibly another double bogey followed at the 17th for the man who had made virtually no error in sixty nine previous holes. Thus Simon Owen, the man who was later to battle the great Jack Nicklaus right to the final hole of the British Open, found himself a surprise winner of the 1978 NZPGA Championship of New Zealand.

CHARLES’ PUTTER FAILS TO DELIVER IN 1980

An air shot on the 15th hole when his swing encountered a small branch on his downswing cost Roger Davis, the man with his name on his socks, sole custody of the lead after round one of the 1980 NZPGA Championship. His 66 matched that of Kim Southerden, son of Ernie who was a three-time winner of the New Zealand Professional Championship during its earlier match play era. Ominously poised behind the leaders were Simon Owen and Bob Charles on 67 along with Rick Barker, later to become resident professional at Tauranga. American Mike Krantz and Billy Dunk scored 68s.

Charles put a break on the field with a second round 63 that featured eight birdies. Owen and Southerden held on three strokes back while Davis, Krantz, and Barker were still well in contention. Charles increased the lead with a third round 64 that might well have been even better. Playing partner Mike Krantz was quoted as saying: “I think Charles could beat the world on this course”. Record books were being consulted to see if anyone had bettered 16 under par after three rounds and to see what Charles might need to do in the final round to better existing 72 hole records. Charles’ putting, for which he was renowned throughout the golfing world, was on target during these three rounds and he needed just one further birdie in the final two holes of that third round to join Brask, Nagle, and Good as record holders on 61.

To the disappointment of the large gallery, and probably his own, though he showed no outward sign of that, Charles failed to find the green at either hole to drop strokes at each. His 64 still

left him five strokes clear of Davis (65) and seven clear of Krantz (66) and Owen (68). Barker continued his strong run with a third sub-par round taking him to seven under with a round to go.

Starting the final round Charles had two goals: To break Mike Souchak’s best ever 72 hole total of 257, and to win a tournament in the fourth decade in which he had played Championship golf, his first success having come in the 1954 New Zealand Open at Heretaunga. Birdies at the two opening holes suggested both goals could well be achieved but it was not to be. Another two birdies and a single dropped shot made up a 67, a good closing round in most circumstances but disappointing on this particular day.
