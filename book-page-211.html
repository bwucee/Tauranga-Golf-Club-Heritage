Personal experiences

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 211 / 305Next page →

On Tuesdays and Fridays Grant would change from his big work boots to his sand shoes or “running shoes” as he called them and mow the greens with the Overgreen mower which he had to walk behind. This would take virtually the full day so was done only in preparation for Ladies’ day on the Wednesday and for the men on Saturdays. There was no possibility of mowing the morning of competition. For a few weeks leading up to the Spalding Masters, and later the PGA, the greens would be mown daily and members would certainly notice the very significant improvement in speed and trueness. While professionals such as Bob Charles claimed these greens were as good as anything in the country it should be remembered that they played them in January when the greens would clearly have been much better than in the winter months when the local season was in full swing.

We always found it ironic that the course was virtually deserted from November to March, the period in which the greens and fairways were at their best.

The bunkers were filled with fine pumice sand that we dug from a pit located at the back of the fourth tee. This we did by loading the old Model T Ford shovel by shovel full. On Wednesdays the ladies would not realise we were up in the pit behind them as they teed off and we would hear some rather unladylike language if their shots did not go to plan. The gully was of course notorious for the problems it caused, especially to those trying to earn their handicaps. There was no automatic handicap allotted to women at that time. Unlike men who were given a 24 handicap at the outset whether they could play

to it or not, the women had to earn their initial handicap by playing to 36 or better. Until they achieved that women players would be in a state of limbo and many an attempt to qualify foundered in the gully or in the bunkers surrounding the green. The pumice in the bunkers was light and fluffy and good to play out of when dry but when wet it packed down like cement. We would then plough it up with the little Gravely tractor and in doing so a bit of clay would be added to the pumice with the result that the next time it rained the cementing effect would be even worse.

We needed to add new sand fairly frequently and it was hard work.

The Gravely was a marvellously versatile workhorse. We used it with a variety of attachments to stir up the bunkers, to mow the heavy rough in places such as the bottom of the gully, to rotary hoe the ground in new tee and green construction, and to pull a small coring machine. Grant was an ingenious man and he developed a rotary broom, driven off the Gravely, for sweeping corings into rows for easier removal. Another machine he came up with was a groover that he made by adapting a Morrison reel mower for the purpose. It worked very well, though labour intensive. At that time we would groove the greens twice a year then over sow them with the standard 3:1 Brown Top/Chewings Fescue Mix.

With improvement in the science of turf management, led by the newly established New Zealand Institute of Turf Culture based at Palmerston North, and the installation of an effective greens irrigation system the need for reseeding gradually disappeared. The new watering system must have been a great relief to Grant who had been in the habit of driving back to the course at night to shift sprinklers.

Another machine I recall was a vicious looking spiking machine. This was developed in conjunction with Edward’s Engineering, a local company run by prominent member Roy Edwards. His widow Joyce is a Life Member of the Club and a wonderful example of how the game of golf can keep you young in spirit.
