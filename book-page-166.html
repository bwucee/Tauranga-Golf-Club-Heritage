Resident professionals

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 166 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

James work and other experiences in America brought him into contact with a number of well known personalities including Jack Dempsey, the great heavyweight boxing champion known as the ‘Manassa Mauler’, Jack McDonald the superb sprinter termed the ‘Australian Blue Streak’, and Tommy Armour, then the most respected golf teacher in America. During Jim’s time in the Shoe Store in New York Damon Runyon, the renowned storyteller would often visit the shop to sit in a big old leather chair and ‘spin the breeze’ with Uncle Bob as the latter cobbled at his bench. Runyon’s short stories later formed the basis for the Broadway Musical ‘Guys and Dolls’.

One day an American friend who had recently returned from a trip to New Zealand said to Jim: “New Zealand is the place of the future. That is where you should go”. Soon afterward an advertisement appeared in an American publication seeking applications for the position of Professional at the Poverty Bay Golf Club. Jim applied, was duly appointed and, in 1929, he ended his nine year sojourn in the USA to set sail for a country he knew very little about. He spent seven years in Gisborne where, in 1934, he married local lass Margaret McIldowie. Soon afterward, while visiting Rotorua, he met with some Tauranga golfers who enticed Jim to move to Tauranga to take up the position of Professional there.

The Rotorua Golf Club course at Arikikapakapa was a favourite with Jimmy. On one occasion he stood on the ninth tee and said to Margaret who was caddying for him: “If I can par this hole I will be out in 30.” He promptly birdied that beautiful par three to be out in 29. We never did hear what happened in the second nine holes.

It must have been very difficult to build a business as a Club professional in those times. The great depression was still casting a long shadow over the World economy, club memberships were not large, and the course was only open for eight or nine months each year. James supplemented his income by giving lessons to Te Puke members in addition to serving his home club though the trip to Te Puke took a good deal longer than it does today. The Mowat farm and orchard, now the site of Tauranga Girls’ College, contributed further to the needs of the growing Mowat family.

In 1940, with the Second World War in full swing and less and less golf being played, James decided there was little point in persevering in the role of Professional and submitted his resignation. Soon afterward he applied to be reinstated as an Amateur. This was granted by the Golf Association on condition that he have no association with golf in any manner for a stand down period of five years, a condition much more stringent than those that prevail in this modern era. Following this enforced withdrawal, broken only by clandestine lessons in the back paddock for some of his old pupils who became desperate for guidance, James resumed life as an Amateur golfer.

Along with Margaret he participated fully in club activities, including committee work, and played regularly two or three times a week.

Jimmy Mowat was one of many memorable characters to have graced our links. Renowned for the quality of his short game he holed more than his fair share of full iron shots, sometimes after asking an incredulous companion to hold the flag for him. Surprisingly he was never to score a ‘recognised’ hole in one in spite of achieving the feat several times when playing unaccompanied rounds during his time
