Staying together at the Ngongataha Hotel enabled great team spirit to develop and, having seen off the challenges of Waiotapu and Kaingaroa on the Saturday, it was only necessary to take two wins from the highly rated Opotiki team on the Sunday to secure the Pennant. Although the top pair from Opotiki played exceptional golf to go through the weekend undefeated our other four came through to gain the win. Neville Robinson memorably summed up the weekend when he commented: “This tournament has been my Freyberg Rose Bowl. I will never reach that level of play but being a member of a team at my own level, and helping that team succeed, has been the highlight of my golfing life”.

He spoke for all the team. THE LABOUR WEEKEND TOURNAMENT The Tauranga Open Championship was first played in 1934. From the start it was a three-day event played over Labour Weekend. The Saturday play featured two rounds of stroke play. These served as qualifying rounds to sort the field into groups of 16 to contest match play over the following two days. The first sixteen qualified on gross to form the championship division, the remainder on nett scores. All contestants were assured two matches while those defeated on the Sunday afternoon round could play in a Stableford competition on the Monday morning should they so wish. For many years the second sixteen became the ‘Calcutta’ flight with spirited bidding taking place on the Saturday evening as individuals and syndicates vied with one another to secure the most favoured ‘horses’.

In later years it became apparent that some players were setting themselves to qualify in this second sixteen in order to be in a position to lay bids upon themselves. Consequently the organisers altered the conditions so that a draw was made after the qualifying rounds to establish which of groups two or three would form the Calcutta group. This partially thwarted those ‘setting’ themselves to profit from a sudden show of form beyond that which their handicap indicated they were prone to display.

Labour weekend had long had the reputation of featuring severely inclement weather so competitors had to be prepared for wind and rain, especially during the qualifying rounds on the Saturday. The weather could generally be expected to improve on the latter days, the finals almost invariably being played in glorious conditions. One year in the early seventies visitors and locals alike arrived on the course early on Saturday morning to find it virtually unplayable owing to very heavy overnight rain that continued to fall. Auckland players in particular, accustomed as they were to their courses being closed for days on end following such rainfall, suggested that they might as well return to their hotels as there would be no play that day.

“Stay” we assured them. “The rain will stop shortly and the course

will dry out rapidly. We will then start after lunch and reduce qualifying to just one round”. They looked at us as though we were several shillings short of the full quid, they looked at the lakes of water on the eleventh and eighteenth fairways, they looked at one another and they shook their heads in the certain knowledge there would be no play today. How wrong they were. By eleven am the sun was out, by noon the field was away from the first tee, and all signs of water had gone from the worst affected fairways by the time they were reached. From that year on the format of the tournament never again featured two rounds on the Saturday.

The single qualifying round later in the day proved very popular, especially amongst those who were enabled to travel from their homes that morning and thus save a night’s accommodation.
