The rules of the game are under constant revision and there have been dramatic changes over the years. The elimination of the Stymie is well known. Less well known is that prior to the early Fifties the penalty for a lost or out of bounds ball was only that you had to put another ball into play (count the stroke only). My Dad and others found it very hard to accept that you now had to penalise yourself both stroke and distance. They considered this a very harsh penalty for a poorly played stroke.

I recall Peter Thomson playing an exhibition match around 1953. He was the new British Open Champion and his opponent that day was Alex Murray. Thomson had a long, upright, elegant swing and he played some glorious shots though he was no match for Murray that day. On the 8th Murray found trouble on the left and had to chip out just past Thomson’s drive. Thomson then played a short iron close to the pin only to see Murray hole his 3rd stroke and win the hole when Thomson could not sink the birdie putt. At the 13th Thomson’s drive was longer than anyone could recall seeing. He then hit a three iron across the corner to the edge of the green, an achievement unheard of in those days.

I remember Dad dropping a pine cone beside the fence to mark the distance the drive had travelled. The most remarkable memory I have is of an event on the 15th. Thomson drove into the edge of the Lawsoniana trees that then lined the left boundary. His ball lay in some longer grass right by the edge. As he went to chip sideways his club slid down a low branch he had apparently not seen and he completely missed the ball. His comical look of total amazement was matched a minute later with one of sheepish embarrassment when, after successfully chipping out, he had to go to Murray and confess he now lay three.

At the speeches later Thomson praised the course as being the best groomed in all of New Zealand. I was impressed but my father said that he probably said that of all the courses he visited in order to please the locals. Peter Thomson went on to claim further British Open titles and become recognised as a great champion. From our point of view, if not from their’s, it is sad that the super stars of the game no longer have to tour the small clubs of the World in order to cash in on their success.

I saw Thomson again in the late Sixties when he played in the PGA Championship at the Mount. He had filled out somewhat with age and his swing was more round arm in the style of Mike Souchak. I was playing too but I did not have the nerve to approach him and ask him if he remembered the exhibition match at Tauranga and especially that air shot!

I witnessed two holes in one while caddying for Dad. Mick Cambie achieved the feat into a gale on

the 14th one Sunday afternoon. Mick was a big wide shouldered bloke who thrashed at the ball with tremendous power. On the tee he addressed the ball by lifting the club head a foot or so off the ground then tapping down behind the ball before making a series of such taps, each about half the height of the previous lift. When these taps finally ceased he would freeze for an age before launching with all his might at the ball. The distance achieved was often spectacular, the direction frequently wayward. For the hole in one he played a three iron under windy conditions that were such as to leave other players short when playing the driver.
