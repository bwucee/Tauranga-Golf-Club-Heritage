Post war revitalisation

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

After the war the returning service men and women were very enthusiastic in their desire to return to the pleasures of peacetime life as rapidly as possible and golf rated highly amongst those pleasures. As a result there was a concerted desire to restore the course to its full eighteen holes and, aided by a healthy increase in membership, this was soon achieved. By 1951, only six years after the cessation of hostilities, membership had grown to the unprecedented figure of 416. The old clubhouse soon proved woefully inadequate to cater for such a large and active membership and plans were made to finance a new brick building to be sited adjacent to the first tee.

Four hundred debentures at £10 each were sold and a building committee of Alan Davidson, Dave Cambie, and Lionel Smith was empowered to supervise construction that was to be achieved at a cost not to exceed £5,700. The contract was let to a club member, Mr Beck, at what was considered the bargain price of £1/8/6 ($2.85) per square foot. This would be $20.54 per square metre in today’s units. This price allowed the project to be completed under budget to the great satisfaction, and no doubt relief, of the building committee. The old wooden building was advertised for sale at a price of £500 but when it was found it could not be moved in one piece the buyer negotiated a reduction and the building was moved to Pyes Pa where it again became a domestic home.

The new clubhouse was opened in 1954. While the final outcome was widely admired the project, like most projects run for the benefit of a large group of members, had not been free of controversy. Chief amongst these was the siting of the building. There was a strong move to utilise the area to the west of the putting green on the rise where the right-hand third tee is now located. This would have provided a more panoramic view of the course and left the Moreton Bay to command the area behind the first tee. Apart from the problems this tree now causes by dropping litter on the clubhouse and surrounds it is probably true that the right location was chosen.

The latest incarnation of the clubhouse, built upon the foundations of the 1954 building, provides a much better view of the course and surrounding landscape than was ever previously evident.

The new clubhouse was but one factor in the continued growth of membership and club usage. The exposure of the game on television, the advent of a healthy professional tournament circuit in New Zealand, and the rapid improvement in golf course maintenance led by the Turf Research Institute of New Zealand all contributed to the development of the game nation-wide. For the Tauranga Golf Club this renewed interest reached its peak in the sixties and seventies when player pressure became so great that membership had to be restricted. Full men’s membership was capped at 335 with just five discretionary places available for players arriving from out of town with national handicaps of 4 or less.

The Club’s Bank manager or the CEO of our neighbour, the Racing Club, would also gain membership under these discretionary places.

While this restricted membership was a necessity, if play was not to become so congested as to be unpleasant for all, it was very unpopular with keen players transferring to Tauranga from other parts of New Zealand, a trend that was occurring at an ever-accelerating rate and continues into the 21st Century. In an attempt to create opportunities for prospective members to obtain some form of membership new categories of male membership were introduced. Older, less active members were encouraged to move to the new B category that allowed play on six days a week excluding Saturdays.
