Greenkeeping

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 177 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

There could be no more important function in the management of a golf club than that of ensuring that the physical environment should satisfy the aspirations of the majority of members. As all who have sat in a clubhouse for any length of time will realise it is very difficult to achieve that end. The “experts” who are eager to inform all who will listen as to what is wrong with the way the course is managed are legion. In spite of this, and in spite of the difficulties presented through lack of surplus funding to achieve all that might be desired, conditions on the majority of New Zealand courses are remarkably good.

In this regard Tauranga is no exception and it is probably true that members have enjoyed above average conditions compared to those prevailing elsewhere in New Zealand throughout most of our history.

In the early years green keeping was very much a matter of keeping the grass growing and regularly mown. The machinery used to achieve this was not specifically designed for golf course work with the result that greens tended to be well-grassed but slow, fairways weedy and stalky, the rough long, thick and difficult. Bunkers, where they existed, were generally unkempt and highly variable in playing characteristics. On many courses greens were mown just once a week, usually on a Friday ready for the weekend play.

While Mr Coles, Mr Reeves, and other early members of staff did a very good job with the equipment and knowledge they had at the time it was not until the appointment of Grant Silvester in 1951 that Tauranga Golf Club entered the modern era of scientific Golf Course Management. That aspect of our history, and the history of Golf Course Management in New Zealand is best presented by detailing Grant’s career in the service of the Club.

GRANT SILVESTER

Grant Silvester took over the role of head greenkeeper in 1951 and remained in the role for a further 21 years. Up until that time the role had been seen more as that of a grass cutter and general labourer than that of a manager of fine turf. The work of Sir Bruce Levy, Ernie Madden, and others, which ultimately led to the publication, in 1949, of the booklet, “The Construction, Care and renovation of the Golf Course” marked the beginning of a new era in turf culture. It is to the great credit of Grant Silvester that he very quickly recognised the value of the research that had been done, and continues to be done, through the work of the New Zealand Institute of Turf Culture.

Coming to the job with no preconceived ideas Grant proved the right man in the right place at the right time. Prior to the work of Levy and his associates well meaning farmers and others had dominated Greens Committees. These men promoted grass growth through the use of lime and superphosphate, as they would have done in growing pasture. This proved to be the antithesis of what was required and greens throughout the country suffered from a host of deficiencies until staff and administrators gradually began to adopt the “acid theory” of fine turf culture identified through the research. It was the beginning of a movement that would see rapid improvement in playing conditions on courses throughout New Zealand and it is to Grant Silvester and the members of the Greens committees of the time that we owe gratitude for ensuring that Tauranga Golf Club was in the vanguard of that movement.
