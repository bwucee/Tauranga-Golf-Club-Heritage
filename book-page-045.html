Disposal of corings from the greens over a couple of seasons has now eradicated that reminder of days gone by. During those war years nine holes were retained and kept in remarkably good order by the sole green keeper, Mr Reeves, an outstanding man who worked under very difficult conditions with limited equipment. The racecourse and its buildings were headquarters for the National Reserve and many members underwent training there. Some found the training exhausting but not so Ted Bartlett, the oldest man in camp, or Bob Gray, each of whom would run around the race track before Reveille each morning.

Years later I enjoyed playing some rounds with Ted when he was quite an elderly gentleman and I can testify that he was still very fit and a real pleasure to play with. He had genuine zest and enthusiasm for the game. I especially appreciated the manner in which Ted accepted myself and other juniors as fellow players on an equal footing with himself in the days when junior members were considered by many others to be an unnecessary evil on the course. Ted loved to hit the ball hard and appeared not to change his approach as age caught up with him.

Des Roberts some 50 years back played from the third tee only to see a lad come out of the trees, pick up his ball, and head towards the stream from which we now get our irrigation water. Giving chase Des ran the lad down then marched him back to the green where he made him drop the ball. Des then two putted for a hard earned ‘Birdie three’.

Full marks for energy and enterprise if not for strict adherence to the rules.

His golfing friends fondly knew Frank Willoughby as the ‘Sand Shoe Champion’. One day Frank used a similarly free rule interpretation after hooking his ball from the 14th tee onto the lawn in front of the County Council Building. Armed with his trusty wedge Frank scaled the fence, crossed the road, played a lovely shot across the traffic to the heart of the green, holed the putt, and claimed a par. He definitely deserved one.
