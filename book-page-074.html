The life of any living system is subject to the process of evolution. The game of golf may not be considered to be a living system and yet it does display many of the characteristics of life. Those early veteran golfers first decided to satisfy their mid-week craving for a bit of competition by playing simple ‘haggles’ or ‘Nassaus’ as the Americans call them. These would usually be played for a maximum of ‘a bob, a bob, a bob’. As weekends merged into the working week the attraction of a break in the normal Monday to Friday routine became more and more attractive to a wider range of working people.

Consequently those who could do so began to ‘put a line through’ Thursday afternoon appointment diaries in order to be free to head for the greener acres of the golf course.

In the mid-eighties Bruce Simister became one of these as he found running a retail business left little opportunity to enjoy golf on a Saturday. On joining play on Thursday afternoons Bruce was disappointed to find that those participating did so in regular fours that played together week after week. Nothing wrong with that perhaps but would it not be a good opportunity to meet new people and discuss some of the issues that might arise in business? It did seem that more could be made of the afternoon. The idea began to spread until there were a dozen or so who were keen to form drawn fours and have a

little competition between the groups so established. At first the winning team would receive $20 from each of the losing teams. This was fine when there were only two or three teams but as the concept became more popular the reality that one team got $100 or more while the rest got nothing became a little difficult to accept with good grace. Consequently a scale of reward based on the number of teams in the competition was instigated. Someone then found a 10-litre paint tin in the locker room and knocked a hole in the top so each potential participant could place his named ball in this bucket. Prior to tee off these would be drawn in groups of four from the bucket and away the teams would go from

midday onward. So began the ‘Ball in the Bucket’ brigade, the same system, and perhaps the same bucket still being used today.

To add variety to the team competition a mathematical formula was instigated with fresh combinations being employed from one week to the next. Everything is based on Stableford points earned at each hole. One week the formula might require the first two scores to be added, the result then multiplied by the third. On another occasion the first and second scores are added, likewise the third and fourth, the two sums then being multiplied together. The variations are endless and often challenge the mathematically impaired. There have been those who swear that multiplying one by zero gives a different result from that obtained by multiplying zero by one, while the ‘order of operations’ rule taught to every third form student is a great unknown to many a successful businessman.

While the team entry remains at $20 the potential financial wins and losses have escalated as the variety of ‘extras’ within the group has expanded. The basic ‘Nassau’ became $5, $5, $5, while a range of ‘Stars’ gradually evolved. Gaining a star added 50c to the pair’s total earnings. These stars included the usual birdies, weasels, and nearies, and in addition such exotic creatures as gritties, fannies, and jesneys.
