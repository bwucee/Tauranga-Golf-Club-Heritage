While physical changes to the design of the links have had a significant influence on both the challenge and the enjoyment of playing the course they have been overshadowed by aspects of course management that have evolved over the years. In those first years after the Club moved to the Racecourse Reserve there were no defined fairways and grazing animals made up most of the ‘greens staff’. Fences surrounded the greens. The greens were the only areas mown regularly, and that perhaps just once each week. The Bay of Plenty Times of March 30th 1936 reported that the course was open for play, that the fairways would be mown from April 1st in accordance with the club’s arrangement with the tenant, and that the fences surrounding the greens would be removed.

Clearly the stock would also be removed. As can be imagined playing conditions would have been very much inferior to those enjoyed by members today. The greens, following a summer of uncontrolled growth, would suddenly be cut back thus producing slow stubbly surfaces that would gradually improve with regular mowing. The rest of the course would get an occasional mowing with Mr McKinlay’s horse drawn hay mower.

Soon after the above item appeared in the ‘Times’ four groups of members representing Bankers, Commercial Travellers, Loan and Mercantile, and Insurance Brokers undertook to enlarge and remake certain greens and an intense competition was soon underway as to who would prove to have the best techniques and achieve the best results. This was perhaps not the way to get a set of greens that played consistently throughout the course but was a big step forward in the evolution of the course.

Keith Page heard that Tom Bloor had purchased a 5hp rotary hoe, the first to be demonstrated in Tauranga, so he pressured Tom into rotary hoeing his green, the 10th. This hole was subsequently named ‘K D Page’ in Keith’s honour. It was claimed that Keith practically slept beside his green to ensure everything went to plan.

The other teams were initially disadvantaged in that they had to dig over their greens with spades. Loan and Mercantile, represented by Cyril Jordan and Ron Mirrielees were claimed to have an unfair advantage because of their superior agricultural knowledge and access to fertiliser and other requirements for their green, the 16th. Perhaps the claim was true for the 16th remains an excellent green and has suffered no serious problem from disease or burnout in spite of being at the most distant corner of the course and amongst the last to receive a reliable water supply. The K D Page green on the other hand became something of a mystery as, for many years it was a ‘green of two halves’, the front being almost pure paspalum which was very green and provided quite a good playing surface in summer, while the rear half was the standard browntop/fescue mix.

Perhaps only Keith knew how this came to be. It was interesting to see how the paspalum gradually disappeared to be replaced by the finer grasses under the influence of modern cultural techniques without the need to kill it off with herbicides. Such experiences vividly demonstrate the way the soil environment influences the species that grow in it.
