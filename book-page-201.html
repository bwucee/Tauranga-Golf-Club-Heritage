As each new score was entered the oldest would be dropped and the handicap, if necessary, recalculated. Arnold Shanks took over the role of keeping track of all this as part of his many duties as Barman and House Manager. It became a common sight to see Arnold poring over cards and record books covering the entire pool table and surrounding area. A number of modifications and improvements have been made to the system since the first version was introduced but the essential basis of the best 10 of the last 20 rounds still applies ensuring that handicaps change quickly so as to more accurately represent current form.

No handicapping system will ever achieve the ultimate aim of placing everyone on an equal footing because we humans are far from perfect. There will always be those seeking to gain an edge and Tauranga, like every other Club around the Country, has had its share of those who have found ways to do this. They soon become well known, if not well respected.

In 1995 a complicated system called Calculated Course Rating (CCR) was introduced in an attempt to equate scores made on different days on the same course by allowing for changing conditions. This system proved totally impractical, was widely ‘honoured in the breach’, and finally abandoned to the relief of all who attempted to operate it. The next genuine step forward was made in September 2000 with the adoption of the ‘Slope’ system, the aim of which is to allow players from different Clubs to compete on an equal footing irrespective of the course they are playing. Essentially the system measures how much more difficult a particular course is for the ‘Bogey Player’ compared to the ‘Scratch Player’.

This measurement results in a theoretical slope line running between the scratch player and the “bogey” player that is then extrapolated to include players on handicaps beyond those limits. It is interesting to note that Tauranga’s Slope Ratings of 117 for men and 120 for women, as measured from the standard tees, are each slightly above the average rating of 113. The course is clearly not as “easy” as some have suggested over the years.

For many years it seemed apparent that Tauranga members were not well served by the Course rating System. Certainly the Club’s record in Bay of Plenty Intermediate and Junior Pennant fixtures supported this view. The introduction of the Slope System appears to have brought with it a more equitable comparison between the courses in the Bay, and probably beyond. The Club does seem to have been more competitive at these levels in recent years. Course ratings depend upon the subjective decisions of those appointed to do the job and are thus subject to human error so there is no way of confirming or disproving any assumption people may make about the relative fairness between ratings.

There will always be discrepancies, both real and imaginary, but these will reduce as rating committees become more experienced and skilled. The combination of the USGA Handicapping System, The Slope System, and accurate Course Rating, have gone a long way towards providing a “level playing field” for all registered golfers. The amalgamation of the National organisations controlling Men’s and Women’s Golf in New Zealand has resulted in a Handicapping and Course Rating system common to both. The development and widespread adoption of computerised handicapping and card analysis has made what were onerous and time-consuming tasks quite simple.
