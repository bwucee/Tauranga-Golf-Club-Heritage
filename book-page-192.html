Trees

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

Courses where they may be used to enhance the strategic design of particular holes, to provide shelter and shade, to beautify the landscape, and to encourage bird and insect life. Unfortunately many a course in New Zealand has been designed as a series of tree-lined avenues with little attempt to provide variation through the use of strategically placed bunkers, mounds, or other features. Such courses may be challenging but they do lack the variety that distinguishes those courses that provide the most stimulating golfing experience. Tauranga is not free of such holes but fortunately the topography of the course lends itself to a good measure of variety between holes and within single holes.

Careful planning and wise future development are necessary if the “member friendly” character of the course is to be preserved for the benefit of future generations. Sensible selection, placement, and culture of trees will be an important aspect of those aspirations. Mistakes have been made in the past. Some trees such as the Casuarinas planted to the right of the second fairway are totally unsuited to the role for a variety of reasons. They are not attractive specimen trees, they shed acidic litter that kills the grass around their base and, as they age, they spread large shallow roots over a wide area. Similar criticism could be made of Pinus radiata, Macrocarpa, and others that are far too common on our courses.

Alex Glasgow, a senior advisor with the New Zealand Sports Turf Institute said in a paper on Golf Course Tree Planting that “the only good thing about yellow macrocarpas is that they don’t live too long and most of them are now coming to the end of their lives”. The Japanese flowering cherries that attract large numbers of Tuis in Springtime unfortunately are readily spread in the droppings of those beautiful birds. As a result they threaten to become a major weed problem if that is not already the case. How much better it would have been had Kowhai and Kaka Beak been planted instead to encourage the Tuis. There are many other attractive native trees that may well have found a place on the course though hindsight is indeed a wonderful thing and perhaps Mr Mirrielees, and those who followed, had little choice but to use what was readily available and affordable at the time.

For many years any tree growing on the course appeared to be treated as though it was untouchable. This was unfortunate, as trees on a golf course primarily need to serve the needs of the game. While well placed, well groomed, trees are an important adjunct to the landscape of a golf course the course is not, nor should it be seen to be, a Botanical Garden. In many cases early specimens were allowed to grow as they wished with the result that many grew into multi-trunk specimens that cannot now be readily trained to gain pleasing shape and form though Arbor Care and our own chain saw artiste, Jim Swainson, have made valiant efforts to do so.

Many of these trees, especially the less desirable conifers, are past their best days and it is to be hoped that

a continued program of removal and replacement will see an improvement in both the aesthetic, and strategic value of trees on the course.

In earlier times branches were allowed to grow down to ground level making it difficult to get under them, let alone play any form of recovery shot should you be unfortunate enough to be a few yards off line. In other situations trees close to greens and tees caused severe shading that led to weak turf subject to insect and disease damage. Happily a more enlightened approach has been taken in more recent times with real benefit to both the quality of the turf and to the enjoyment of the player.
