DAVID MOWAT REMEMBERS

My earliest memories of the Club are of hunting for golf balls in the hydrangeas that fronted the clubhouse where it faced the 18th green. My brother Robert and I would be waiting for our father as he enjoyed the after match conviviality with his friends in the old wooden cottage that served as clubhouse. As children we were not permitted within its hallowed walls. Sometimes we would go further afield in search of balls and tees, the latter being quite common near the men’s tees but rare indeed near the ladies’. Strange that. At home we would use our finds in company with a couple of old irons to hit balls around our paddocks which now make up the Girls’ College at Gate Pa.

When I was about twelve my parents signed me up as a junior member though I was far more interested in cricket, tennis, rugby and yachting at that stage. In any event juniors were not made very welcome at that time and not for quite a few years into the future. My father, the first professional to the club but then back in the amateur ranks, was keen to see a change in that attitude which is why he signed me up. I caddied for my father or one of his friends on occasion. This was before the days of trundlers and we were especially called upon for tournament days when carrying your bag through 36 holes was a daunting prospect.

My first real commitment to the game came in 1957 when I took a year off university in the belief that

I needed to reawaken my enthusiasm for study. A vacancy arose as assistant to Grant Silvester on the green keeping staff and I gained the position through the influence of my father. Grant had been our neighbour at Gate Pa for many years and my father some years previously had suggested to Grant that he would be ideally suited to the green keeping role. Grant and the committee concurred and so began a significant era in the development of the course. I recall working with Grant with a good deal of pleasure. Grant could be a hard task master but very fair and patient, in passing his knowledge and skills on to me and to others.

Though Grant had come to the job with little in the way of the special knowledge required of the modern green keeper he was practical, adaptable, and a ready learner. He, along with Charlie Leach of Taupo, was to become instrumental in raising the standards of green keeping throughout the Bay of Plenty. While accompanying Grant to meetings of the BOP Green Keepers’ Association it was soon apparent to me that the other members paid a great deal of respect to the views of these two men.

The machinery we had to work with was minimal compared to the specialised and sophisticated machinery operated by our present-day staff. An old Ferguson tractor hauled a set of three gang mowers that I would use four days a week to mow the course from boundary to boundary during the active growth season. There was virtually no rough. For two days I would mow length wise up and down the fairways, then crosswise for another two days. This was necessary to combat the Paspalum stalks that would otherwise have turned the fairways into hay paddocks in appearance. I remember some summers when the stalks did get away and Jimmy Paterson would be ashamed to charge visitors green fees to play the course.

These days it is difficult to find Paspalum anywhere in the fairways and the old local rule allowing Paspalum stalks to be plucked has long since gone from our card. I was very happy doing my mowing job when the sun was shining. On those days I would sing all the popular songs of the day, tunelessly but enthusiastically, as I drove the faithful Fergie around the fairways.
