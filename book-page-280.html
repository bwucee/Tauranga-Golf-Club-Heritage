The second one hundred years, 2014–2114

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 280 / 305Next page →
Original ending: includes an imagined future. The short-course account is followed by predictions written in 2014, not a record of later events. Historical side anecdotes also appear in the printed chapter.
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

The most important debate of the first two decades of the 21st century was related to Global warming. The question was not as to whether it was a reality, but whether it was caused by the activities of humans or was a purely natural phenomenon that had been recurrent throughout the history of the planet. By the year 2020 the vast majority of people had come to accept that anthropogenic activity was clearly the major cause. In light of all the evidence presented to the public and to politicians by scientists it became increasingly difficult to argue that a century and more of intensive livestock farming, forest clearance, fossil fuel use in transport, energy production, and other applications, together with the associated generation of waste products had had little, if any, influence on the state of the atmosphere and the consequent health of the Planet.

The academic debate receded into history as people everywhere realised that fighting the problem, not debating the cause, was essential if a path to a sustainable future was to be found. The dangerous upward trend in Global temperature was finally stopped in the decade of the Forties, but not reversed until much later in the Century.

The Bay of Plenty had been blessed with a climate that was the envy of those in other parts of the Country, and indeed in much of the World. While summer droughts had been an occasional problem in the region they became much more frequent and more intense as the Century progressed. Consequently the decision of the Board back in the Nineties to establish Windsor Green fairways throughout the course proved to be a great investment for the future. These Cynodon fairways continued to thrive and provide a good playing surface in both summer and winter without the need to install fairway watering. Extensive irrigation would not have proved a viable option in any event as legislators were forced to severely restrict water usage as water became an increasingly scarce resource.

By 2020 the Board had reacted to the restrictions on the use of irrigation water by converting all the tees and fairways to Windsor Green and to investigating the availability of a suitable strain of Cynodon for use on the greens should the need to convert them arise. In 2030 the decision was made to go ahead with converting the existing predominately Brown top greens to a very fine leaved Cynodon developed by the New Zealand Institute of Turf Management. While these measures added to the requirement for a greater level of mechanical treatment to greens, fairways, and tees, they allowed members to enjoy a high standard of course presentation even in the driest years.

Throughout its early years the Tauranga course had been considered short by National or International standards. There was little that could be done to change that though the reduced run on the Cynodon fairways certainly increased the effective playing length by a significant margin. The possibility of

developing some additional holes in the Kopurererua Valley had been in mind since late in the 20th Century but the cost and other problems associated with this meant no decision to go ahead had been made by 2020. At that time modern equipment, especially advances in ball technology, had contributed to the relative shortening of all golf courses. Many quite ordinary club golfers were driving the ball far in excess of the distances reached by top professionals in the years when the PGA Championship was held on the Tauranga course some fifty years previously. Fortunately the International administrators of Golf, the Royal and Ancient Golf Club of St Andrews (R and A) and the USGA, finally recognised that technological developments were simply making the game more and more expensive as existing courses proved too short for even the average player.
