Trees

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 189 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

During the depression years of the Thirties then President Mr A J Mirrielees saw an opportunity to use the Government unemployed relief worker scheme to enhance the Golf Course environment through an extensive tree planting program. Ultimately many thousands of trees were planted as shelterbelts, strategic course components, and as purely ornamental trees. Mr Mirrielees worked with those trees that were available to him at the time and in general the selection and placement of these have stood the test of time. Over the years some specimens proved less than ideal and were removed and often replaced with more suitable varieties. Others have died, become diseased, or been damaged or blown over by the strong winds that sometimes sweep the area.

Controversy has arisen over almost all moves to remove any particular tree and yet, once they have gone, they are soon forgotten and people wonder what the fuss was about. The most cosseted tree on the course was perhaps the Japanese Weeping pine (Pinus patula), also known as the Waterfall pine, which once graced the ridge between the first and second fairways. The protective shelters erected around it were blown down, cows trampled it, rabbits burrowed under it, golfers occasionally struck it with ball or club, and mowers knocked bark off it. Despite all these setbacks it flourished and enhanced the ridge for many years until it finally lost a battle with the elements during a storm in 1979.

Bruce Burbery, Murray McDonald, and occasionally others of their friends were early “golf ball entrepreneurs” on the course. As youngsters they would find balls at various popular spots and offer them to members at 1/- for a good one. At this price Dunlop 65s and Spalding Hot Dots were keenly sought. The boys had to be on the alert for, should Grant Silvester sight them, he would chase them from the course, sometimes before they had time to take their prize finds with them. In time Bruce accumulated enough 1/- pieces to purchase a new Peter Thomson driver from Jim Paterson. Jim encouraged Bruce to try his new purchase on the first tee.

Jim came out of his shop to watch, knowing that he might see something spectacular from the long hitting youngster. The first drive finished Pin high left, the next right. As Bruce prepared to hit a third Noel Hocken appeared from his office. “We do not allow more than two balls from the tees in practice” said Noel in his most formal and forbidding managerial manner. While Jim looked sheepishly the other way Bruce headed up the fairway to retrieve the balls and head to the second tee. Jim, guessing Bruce would not be able to resist the challenge of driving to the second green headed across there. Grant Silvester was on his faithful ‘Fergie’ beside the green so Bruce waited for him to move away but Grant waved him to play.

Bruce drove, the ball carrying far enough to strike the engine cover on the tractor leaving a considerable dent, and flying past Grant’s ducking head. Jim sneaked quietly back to his shop while Bruce anticipated a right bollocking but Grant took it all in good part.
