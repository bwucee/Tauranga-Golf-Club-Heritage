Greenkeeping

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

During the few short years Brendan spent as Course Supervisor at Tauranga he made a tremendous impact on all those with whom he was associated. Not only did he bring a wealth of experience disproportionate to his years, he introduced fresh ideas and methods that continued to influence course conditions and policy long after his departure. Whilst the Club administration would dearly have loved to retain Brendan’s services they were unable to compete with the resources available to the Auckland Golf Club of Middlemore who soon became aware of Brendan’s outstanding ability and made him “an offer he couldn’t refuse”. We could only wish him well and lament that we had not been able to enjoy the benefit of his expertise over a much longer period.

It will be surprising if Brendan does not have a lasting impact on green keeping practise throughout the country.

In 2006 Graeme Duncan was appointed to fill the role vacated by Brendan. Graeme came to us from the home of Golf, Scotland, via Inglewood Golf Club in Taranaki. This was the same club from which we recruited Royce Lahood, a man who contributed so much to our Club while serving in the capacity of Manager from 1970 to 1983. Graeme demonstrated early in his tenure that he had clear ideas on how he wanted the course to develop under his methods and simply got on with the task of achieving those objectives. Again conditions are different here to those in Taranaki or in Scotland. None the less the principles of growing fine turf and maintaining it over extended periods do not differ markedly from place to place.

As Graeme became familiar with the subtle environmental differences of our course that need to be taken into account he grew confidently into the job and soon produced course conditions right up to the expectations of the majority of members. Perhaps he will be most remembered for his bunker construction. While the reconstruction of the bunker fronting the Knoll has added greatly to the frustrations so many of our members experience when playing that hole there is nothing inherently wrong with the bunker or its construction. Any perceived problem really lies in the inadequacy of our techniques to deal with genuine hazards. Shallow bunkers with low lips, as are common on New Zealand courses, hardly constitute hazards.

The first trial of sand imported from outside the course for use in bunkers was made in 1956 when one truckload of river sand was purchased. It was not until 1963 that Papamoa beach sand was finally used in bunkers throughout the course thus relegating the fine pumice previously used to history. It was definitely an improvement though the pumice, when dry and fluffy, had been excellent to play from. Unfortunately it did not remain in that state for long. Sanding of greens was considered over many years before it was finally introduced. Grant Silvester and others had observed that those areas of the greens where players had spread sand when recovering from bunkers appeared to offer a better

environment to Browntop and Chewings Fescue than did other parts of the green. Bruce Levy and his associates had proven the value of sanding on the Hokowhitu greens but would it confer the same benefits under our very different soil and climatic conditions? Michael Nicholson was probably the man who did most to push for the introduction of sanding on our greens. A member of the Greens Committee from 1985 to 1993 Michael had seen the value of incorporating sand into the greens on courses he played at Provincial and International level, both in New Zealand and Overseas. There was plenty of opposition however. The Club had received a report from the New Zealand Institute of Turf Culture advising that the technique would not be suitable on our greens.
