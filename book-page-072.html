Men’s golf

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 72 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

H D (Harold) Brinsden won that first Tauranga Open, following up with a further three successive wins before local man Norman Green, who also won three in a row, broke his dominance. Multiple winners were a feature of the tournament over the succeeding years, Fred Ward winning six times, Don Boone five, Noel Johnson, Owen Kendall, and Michael Nicholson each four times. These were all outstanding players who each represented the Bay of Plenty with distinction on many occasions. Noel Johnson, who learnt his golf in Tauranga before moving to Whakatane, fashioned a remarkable record by winning his fourth title in 1999 twenty-five years after winning his first.

While outstanding championship winners make for an interesting history of the tournament it is often the ‘bit-part’ players who confer upon the event its special quality. This was certainly true of the Labour Weekend Tournament. From the start it attracted an enthusiastic field from a wide range of clubs from Auckland, The Waikato, The King Country, and The Bay of Plenty. The Maungakiekie club of Auckland built their representation in the event to as many as twenty-five over the years, with the same faces appearing year after year. Who from that time would forget the likes of Nick Burmaz, Ian (Mouse) McKendry, Dick Talyancich, Auctioneer supreme Peter McCullough, and their ilk?

The tiny Matiere club from the King Country came in such force that almost their entire male membership must at times have been entrants. The rapport between that club and our own grew to the point where a summer fixture was established with reciprocal visits being organised to play cricket and golf matches between members of the two clubs, the hosts billeting their guests and families for the occasion. Noel Copestake was instrumental in initiating these exchanges. They will be remembered with much pleasure, not least by the children of our members. The children were usually billeted separately from their parents and enjoyed the novel experience of life on a farm and perhaps the freedom from parental supervision.

The Labour Weekend Tournament flourished in an age when life was more relaxed. People seemed to have more leisure time and a greater willingness to travel away from home in order to participate in organised events. Whole families came on these trips, the wives getting together to enjoy their own activities while the men played golf. Social activity involving the men and their wives (the concept of partners being little considered at that time) was catered for in the clubhouse in the evenings.

Calcutta Sweeps, though illegal, were exceptionally popular and usually built into large prize pools. Sometimes it was necessary to advise the likes of Jim Rossiter, Russell Christie, or Bill Graham,

Norman Ducker, Ken Carter, and Peg Copestake all have the distinction of having scored an albatross at the sixth hole. Though the American commentators insist on calling this a double eagle we like the original, more descriptive, term. In any event the feat is a rare and meritorious achievement. Each of these three-under-par scores was recorded using the old equipment and they are especially notable for that reason alone.
