It was typical of him that he had not bothered with taking a drop and trying for a miracle shot to rescue a point.

Another example of Fred’s equanimity that has remained with me happened at the final hole during a Labour Weekend Tournament. At the time important events on the Club calendar such as the Labour weekend Tournament were eagerly anticipated, intensely contested, thoroughly discussed and dissected for months afterwards. In the annals of the Tournament the epic battles between Fred Ward and Andy Fletcher were legendary. A J Fletcher was a talented golfer, a long hitter and a fierce competitor. Regardless of the quality of the field it seemed that Fred and Andy always met in the final at Labour Weekend. The Honours Board reveals that Fred usually prevailed. One year they reached the 18th all square and both made the front right of the green with their second strokes.

Both longish putts finished close to the hole and the gallery anticipated concessions and a move to the first tee to continue the contest. Fred knocked Andy’s ball back to him but Andy made no move to reciprocate whereupon Fred made a quick stroke, and laughed before holding out his hand to congratulate Andy as the ball slipped by the hole. I was horrified that such a mighty encounter could end like that but it was typical of Fred to take defeat in that manner. On another occasion the pair went to the 3rd extra hole before Fred secured another title when Andy duffed several successive strokes in the gathering gloom.

The veteran golfers of the time were my friends and many gave me welcome encouragement. If we were playing on a Sunday they would often allow me to drop a ball and play the last couple of holes. Dick Phelan was one of those men. Dick had established the Tauranga Rimu Company and had thus been responsible for giving Tauriko its name for you won’t find that word in any Maori dictionary. Harry Smith and Maurice Bradmore were always there with an encouraging word and they let me have a hit when the opportunity arose. Ron Galloway was a very good single figure player. He had a new maroon

Vanguard motorcar while George Thorne had an identical car, even to the colour. There was a story of the two of them racing them up Waihi Road on their way home from golf but stories like that were not for a boy’s ears and I heard no more. Alan Davidson was a young man at the time and regarded as a promising player who was just starting to make his mark in Club affairs. He too took an interest in me at a time when most players would have known who I was but chose to ignore my presence as I dragged my father’s heavy home made trundler around the course.

Lew Gamman was an exception, always friendly and chatty. Visits to town were not complete without a visit to the Sports Depot in Wharf Street where Alan McBride or, more likely, Lew would sell me a small piece of Meccano while chatting to Dad.

Other players I remember include Paul O’Brien who played off a six handicap and had a very smooth swing and Toby Green, then the longest hitter in the Club. I recall Toby driving the 10th green, an unheard of feat at the time. George Harrison and Dave Cambie used to have chipping competitions down at the end of Grace Road where they lived. They would chip across the road, round and even over the houses, and out onto the harbour. George was a keen fisherman and he would go around to the newly reclaimed swamp that is now Memorial Park in order to practise his casting. Once George gave us some nylon line, the first we had ever seen.
