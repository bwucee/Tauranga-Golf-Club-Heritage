Setting the scene

The earliest written record of Tauranga is attributed to Judge Wilson who served as Commissioner of Lands for the Tauranga District in the latter years of the 19th Century. In his ‘Sketches of Ancient Maori Life and History’ he records that when the canoe Takitimu, part of the first group of canoes to arrive from Hawaiki, called at Tauranga the newcomers found the district to be occupied by two tribes of aboriginal people known as the Purukapenga and the Ngamaramas. Purukapenga may be translated as ‘Full Net’ and so is in accord with James Cook’s observation that this was a place of abundant resources. Despite the obvious attractions of the area the immigrants aboard Takitimu decided it would be wise to sail further south in search of less populated land.

Nearly 600 years then passed prior to the arrival of the first European missionaries during which time numerous tribal battles were waged in attempts, both successful and unsuccessful, to lay claim to this most desirable of locations. Those early missionary newcomers were very soon supported by a strong contingent of British military forces and the stage was set for the commencement of the wide range of activities that these people from the other side of the world brought with them.

Construction of ‘The Elms’ Mission House began in 1838 and was completed in 1847. This was the home of Archdeacon Alfred Nesbit Brown who lived there until his death in 1884. In 1878 he wrote to a friend in London suggesting to him that he would barely recognise the town since he visited some years earlier. “We now have two large wharves, four weekly steamers, a large public office building, two well-built Banks, three churches, and three hotels. Land values have risen to prices that would astonish you. A quarter acre on the beach recently sold for £1670, he wrote.”

Living today in this modern, rapidly growing city of Tauranga it is difficult to imagine what the district was like at the beginning of the 20th century. Because of the poorly formed un-metalled roads the town was isolated. Access was mainly by steamer from Auckland. Even so there was considerable development as described by the Archdeacon above. The Strand and Devonport Road were thriving shopping and business centres, though life remained sufficiently relaxed that it was not unusual to find ‘gone fishing’ notices posted on shop and office doors. If business was urgent the proprietors were only too happy to be summoned from the waterfront.

The importance of horse-drawn transport was highlighted by the presence of Lane’s livery stables on the corner of Spring Street and Grey Street, where the National Bank now stands, and by the Grey Street Smithy where Arthur Rolfe plied his trade. That same Blacksmith’s shop was still operating in the 1950s with Charlie Haua at the forge. The now demolished Town Hall was built in 1913 where the City Council offices now stand and no less than four hotels, complete with water troughs and hitching rails, competed for custom in the down town area. The Fire Brigade Headquarters, Mechanics Institute, Hammond’s Bakery, and the Bay of Plenty Times Office were prominent buildings in the centre of the township.

Cameron Road was a narrow strip, dusty in summer, muddy in winter, flanked by grassy verges where cows grazed.
