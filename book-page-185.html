The Club is fortunate that incidents of interference through balls being stolen from fairways have been rare over the years but they do happen. On one occasion two visiting competitors in the 28-hole open Tournament had this happen to them on the 10th hole. Not knowing their rights under the rules they returned to the tee and played out the hole. They were of course entitled to replace the moved balls without penalty. The two strokes this cost them meant that one at least missed out on a high placing. Though the Match Committee was sympathetic to their plight there was nothing they could do to alter the outcome as they had ‘put another ball into play under Stroke and Distance’.

The system was installed under the direction of a club member who, though a qualified Civil Engineer, was neither experienced nor expert in the field of water reticulation. The Club were later to learn to their regret that designing and installing a reticulation system is a job for a specialist in the field. For the next 17 years the Greens staff did their best with a system that proved more and more unreliable until it became obvious that the Club would never achieve the standard of course presentation they desired unless they “grasped the nettle” and replaced the system with a modern, professionally designed, reticulation network incorporating top quality components.

Subsequently the Greens Committee submitted a proposal to the Club for the installation of just such a system by the company known as Northern Irrigation at a cost of $196 000. Approval was gained and the system completed in April 1995. It was wonderful to see the coverage of greens, tees and surrounds that eventuated when the system was first switched on and it was not long before the benefits to the turf could be seen. The water system had finally become a major tool in enabling the staff to achieve the ideal environment to suit the growth of quality sports turf. The cost of the system remained a burden to be carried by the membership for a number of years to follow but few would argue the decision to proceed was not a wise one.

There were some problems encountered as more pressure was placed upon the system, the main one being one of “hammering” in the pipes where their laying had not been up to scratch in some areas. This resulted in some blowouts and leakage that frustrated staff and imposed upon their limited labour resources. Fortunately problems became less frequent as solutions were found. The problems that were encountered illustrated once again the fallacy of cutting corners when building something that is expected to perform reliably day-by-day, year-by-year. In this case the problems encountered were probably the result of using non-specialist labour for the actual installation
