While this was unpopular with those placed on waiting lists it was the only practical solution to a problem that could not have been envisaged by earlier administrators. Since the club’s inception those administrators had been intent on building membership, not restricting it.

Surveys of course usage at the peak of demand over this period found that weekly play often exceeded 1000 rounds and few flat periods were seen through the week or through the year. This made it very difficult for the Greens Staff, still only two full time men, to maintain the quality of the greens. In response to this pressure the committee introduced a policy of trying to provide an alternate green, or at least an enlarged green, at each hole where it was practical to do so.

Under this policy the 1st, 2nd, and 15th greens were enlarged while alternate greens were formed at the 3rd, 4th, 11th, and 17th holes. Only that at the 4th hole survives, the others proving both unpopular and difficult to maintain. Later developments saw enlargements to the 5th, 8th, 9th, 10th, and 17th greens while the fortuitous retention of the old 7th green proved to be a wise decision prior to its later abandonment.

While this policy did not achieve as much as had been hoped it did relieve the pressure for a time. The advent of a vastly improved irrigation system allied to continued improvements in green keeping techniques, especially the introduction of sanding following coring, led to very much improved surfaces that are better able to withstand the constant pressure of players and machinery. These advances reduced the need for alternate greens. It is sobering to contemplate that wherever one goes in sport, as in other human activities, there are always ‘experts’ eager to give the benefit of their advice to the professionals employed to do the job. At one stage, there was a tremendous campaign launched by a group of members outside committee to have coring discontinued on the grounds that so much material was being removed, and so little replaced, that the greens were rapidly becoming sunken saucers.

Usually all that such critics achieve is to prove themselves blind to the facts. In the case of coring and sanding the advantages are obvious to anyone who has played the course continually through recent years. This is because good drainage, soil aeration, and the soil environment developed under coring provide conditions that suit the growth of fine leaved grasses. In our case Browntop and Chewings Fescue are the species used to produce quality greens. As for saucer shaped greens resulting from our coring program some may wish it had happened as the hole could then be cut in the centre of the green and all putts would find their way there!

Following the establishment of a Board of Management to administer the financial and amenity functions of club management a course improvement group was empowered to consider possible changes to the course and to establish priorities for this work. As a consequence of their deliberations quite radical changes were made to the 5th, 7th, and 17th holes, together with minor changes to the 10th, 11th and 13th holes. It would be fair to say that the most radical of these changes have not been met with universal approval by members.
