Though there was no mud on the boots and the clothes were tidy working gear these few complainants obviously felt his presence was inappropriate. Although officialdom never told Grant to stay away he did hear there had been a complaint and he reacted by moving his Friday evening custom to the Greerton Hotel down the road. This was all a bit sad and perhaps a sign that things would never be quite the same again.

The story of Commander John Harris, Alan Davidson and his plastic models, and Grant’s endeavour to replicate these on the ground has been told elsewhere. As a member of the Greens Committee I was able to participate from the sidelines in much of this development. While I regret not being present when Commander Harris walked the course I did gain significant insight into course design and construction from being involved, as a member of the Greens Committee, in the follow up over the succeeding months. Grant and Alan were assisted greatly by the experience and skills of local earth moving contractor Garth Renner. Garth had an empathy with them and could interpret what they wanted very readily and he was genuine in his wish to achieve exactly the effect they were looking for.

It was a real team effort with a result that would, I am sure, have pleased John Harris though I don’t think he saw the final outcome. The majority of holes underwent some degree of change. Some bunkers disappeared, others were introduced, mounds appeared in strategic places and greens were extended to provide extra depth. In the opinion of some excellent judges the developments transformed Tauranga into one of the more interesting courses in the country. We simply do not hear complaints from members or from visitors that the course is boring to play as does happen with regard to quite a number of New Zealand courses. The manager of an Auckland representative team once commented to me: “I wonder if your members fully appreciate the course you have here.

Tauranga is really three courses in one. Those first six holes take us down into the valley and out again. They are full of variety, both of terrain and design. The middle six offer a variety of challenges while the final six represent more typical flat country golf”. To add to that description Michael Nicholson, Tauranga’s outstanding amateur, put it this way: “Those final six holes have a way of sorting the men from the boys”. How prescient that comment was can readily be understood by those who had the privilege of watching Michael in the final stages of many of his championship matches, or in watching another outstanding club member in Eddie Burgess come from behind to win another under 23 stroke play title over those last few holes.

Next time you tee up on the second hole take a look at the three Kauri trees on the right front. Back in 1957 the tee was very different from the way it is now. The tee was in two narrow terraces with tall bracken covering much of the area. One day Grant directed me to clear the bracken away from the

Kauris to “release them”. I had barely realised they were there, hidden as they were by the bracken. From the moment of their release from the shade and competition provided by the bracken they began to thrive and we can now see what 50 years of unrestricted growth does for Kauris. Apparently they had been hidden under the bracken for some 20 years since Keith Lusk planted them in the Thirties. Incidentally the women’s tee was then sited on top of the ridge between the first and second fairways some distance forward and to the right of the present position. The outline of the tee can still be seen. Close to that tee was a large blue gum and if you drove up to, or beyond, that tree when playing the first you had achieved a notable feat.
