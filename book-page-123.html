DICK PHELAN

Dick was made a Life Member of the Golf Club and served as Secretary to the Tauranga Veteran Golfers’ Association, as it was then known, from its inception in 1952 until 1956 after which he continued to serve on committee. Dick donated the Phelan trophy for which we still compete. A fine golfer he retained a single figure handicap for many years. It is recalled that one Saturday after holing his final putt on the 18th green he quietly remarked: “Well, I have played to my age”. He was 76 at the time. An indication of Dick’s skill and longevity as a player is evident from the fact that he won the Senior Championship in 1930 and, 47 years later, was runner-up in the Junior Championship to then 16-year old Robbie Meyer.

That 1976 Finals Day was one of the very rare occasions when the weather has been unkind to the finalists with wet, cold, and windy conditions prevailing throughout most of the day. In spite of that, or of the disparity of sixty years in the ages of the two, it was not until the 35th hole that Dick finally succumbed to his young rival.

Dick objected strongly to the term ‘Die-hards’ that was used to identify the over 70 division in most Veterans’ Tournaments and it is for that reason that we still use the term ‘Veteran of Veterans’ in our Tournament.

GEORGE WILLCOCK

George was a kindly and enthusiastic gentleman in the true sense of the word. A past President of the Golf Club, George was made a life member for his services over many years, especially his work in the

role of Honorary Secretary during the War years. The Willcock name is commemorated in the playing of the popular Intermediate Handicap Match Play event for the Willcock Cup as part of the Saturday Club Programme.

One day Grant Silvester said to me: “Keep your eyes open for green tees on the fairways”. I asked what on earth he meant. “Well it is Veteran’s week and there is a placing rule” I was told. I indeed found green tees on some fairways. It seems some of the old chaps had perfected the technique of palming a green tee as they lifted the ball prior to placing it. At the end of the process the ball would be perched on a tee, not resting on the grass. Having played the stroke they would not want to give the game away by bending to retrieve the tee. Holes such as the 11th, the 15th, and the 16th became reachable with a second stroke when played with ‘brassie’ from a teed lie.

Little wonder it was hard to beat these wily characters.
