In an aside to the day’s play Terry Kendall, having just made the cut for the final round, was drawn to play in a twosome at the head of the field. He and his fellow competitor completed their 18 holes in 2 hours 15 minutes with Terry shooting 67 to show it is possible to play quickly and still score well. It must be said neither player was too concerned about their position in the tournament so it was a very relaxed round. On the same subject long serving Greens Superintendent Grant Silvester was interviewed at this tournament on a number of aspects of play and he did comment that he had noticed a significant increase in the time taken to play a round of golf.

“Five years ago a four could get around in little more than three hours and 3 1/2 hours was considered slow”. Grant’s view had some veracity as he was in a position to observe more rounds than practically anyone else over many years. Courses are more crowded these days so it is not realistic to expect to match the times of another age yet it does appear that there is a lot of room for improvement in the way modern club players manage their way around the course. Leaving your clubs in the right place, being ready to play when it is your turn, filling in the card while others are teeing off, saving social gossip for the clubhouse.

These are all ways to save time and thus add to the enjoyment of everyone. Simple course management is perhaps something of a lost art. Four-hour rounds are now perhaps realistic and acceptable, much longer and they cause unnecessary frustration to the majority of the field. It is also apparent that there is now an unwritten law, at least on our course that: “Thou shalt let no other group play through even if you have lost two fairways and are searching for a ball”. This is all really a little sad.

THE JUBILEE NZPGA CHAMPIONSHIP OF 1976

The legendary A J Shaw was a special guest at the 50th Jubilee Tournament staged at Tauranga in January 1976. Andy Shaw won seven New Zealand Open Championships between 1926 and 1936 together with seven Professional titles, the last of these in 1946. Born in Scotland he was a superb striker of the ball and, given the financial opportunity, was considered to have had the game to succeed on the American Tour. In exhibition matches in New Zealand he defeated both Gene Sarazen and Walter Hagen.

Californian visitor Bill Brask enlivened an unusually rainy January day when he equalled Kel Nagle’s record 61 in the opening round. Eagles at 6 and 7 followed by birdies at each of the next three holes took Brask to nine under and the record looked there for the taking with eight holes left to play. Unfortunately

fate, in the form of the weather, played a hand, the round being suspended for an hour as some greens became flooded. When play resumed Brask drove under the Lawsoniana to the right of the eleventh fairway to drop his first stroke. Birdies at 13 and 15 made it ten under but he was only able to score a five on the par 4 seventeenth as the rain poured down and when a birdie putt slipped by on 18 the record was equalled, not broken.
