So it was that my job began a few days earlier than planned. Noel handed me the keys of the bar and said: “This is your bar. You are in full charge”. He did have a spare key of course and I think when I was not there he would nip in for a quick one. That Saturday morning I started my new career. It was fun really, long hours but not hard work once I was organised and before the novelty began to wear off. The committee had made up a time sheet for me. Monday was cleaning up day, 4 hours. Each changing room took an hour or more, then there was cash to tally, the bar to restock, the lounge to vacuum.

If there had been a Sunday tournament or function this latter took at least two hours. Flat out it was a hard four hours work. Theoretically I worked 44 hours a week and had Monday afternoon off together with a full day off on Fridays. For this I received £20/6/0 or £17/7/4 net of tax. This was in 1967. If, as occasionally happened, the course was closed as a result of bad weather I didn’t get paid for the day.

We had no liquor license so the whole set up was illegal but the law turned a blind eye. Sometimes on a Saturday or Sunday evening someone would ring to say there was a traffic officer parked down the road so everyone leaving would be extra careful. There was never a serious accident, only the occasional scrape on the way home. One chap had a minor run in with a tree on the driveway out to Cameron Road. It was difficult to get the bar closed. Even though we were illegal there was an attempt to stick to the six o’clock closing time for licensed premises but there was a hardened core of drinkers, including Noel, who would stay and stay.

I often had trouble getting the slide down and it was often well after 6:30pm by the time I did.

I also said I would do the flowers, a task that had been done off and on by some of the ladies. Somehow it became part of my job as I had plenty of flowers and enjoyed seeing them in the clubhouse. Usually I had three or four large arrangements and they were always admired, even by some of the men. That chore took some time as the flowers had to be picked freshly every day but I soon got into a routine. I did all I could to keep the clubhouse clean and tidy and no one complained in the time I worked there. Much later, on an economy drive, the committee decided the staff were getting too much free service at the kitchen so we were cut back to just morning and afternoon teas.

Previously I had got my lunch free, now I brought sandwiches from home. I did say: “no lunch, no flowers” but after a week I got tired of the bareness and went back to my arrangements.

Gradually conditions in the bar changed. Instead of bottled beer we changed to draught beer in flagons, then to small casks with a pressure pump. Eventually, following the next building extension, two large tanks and a proper pressure system were installed. That made the serving of drinks and the handling easier but increased the cleaning work. I carried a good stock of spirits and soft drinks and graduated from a small refrigerator to a large commercial style model.
