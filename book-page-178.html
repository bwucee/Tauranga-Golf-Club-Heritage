Greenkeeping

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

Obviously not everyone accepted the new philosophy of turf management and there were many ‘clubhouse experts’ who disagreed very vocally with what was being put into practice. Nothing has really changed has it? In desperation Grant agreed to let some of these ‘expert’ grass growers put their theories into practice. The seventeenth green was divided into four quarters, three of which were allocated to different theorists, the fourth remaining under Grant’s management. Within less than twelve months the three sections under amateur control showed poor grass cover, disease, a muddy surface, and other undesirable features. That under professional management exhibited good grass cover with healthy colour and appearance and a good putting surface.

The ‘experts’ were silenced, at least temporarily. Unfortunately there was an ongoing downside to the experiment as, for some years to follow, one could look down upon the green from the ‘Knoll’ and distinguish the four separate sections. Members had to endure varied conditions on the green for quite a lengthy period.

Grant Silvester was brought up on a small farm overlooking the Waimapu estuary and caught many a flounder there. Upon leaving home he set up an orchard and glasshouse property on the part of Courtney Road now known as 22nd Avenue. It was his neighbour Jimmy Mowat who suggested to Grant that he would be ideally suited to the Greenkeeper role that had become available at the Club. After ensuring that he would have some flexibility in meeting the demands of both his orchard and the greenkeeper role Grant accepted the job with some trepidation. He accepted that he had all the

practical skills required but he had no experience of golf, golfers, or the special requirements of sports turf. Having accepted the challenge he took it upon himself to gain the necessary knowledge as quickly as possible. In doing so he became the first at Tauranga, and amongst the first Nationally, to recognise the value of the research done on turf culture. Encouraged by Alan Davidson, Jimmy Mowat and others, Grant visited other clubs to study their methods. He went to greenkeepers’ conferences, often accompanied by Greens Committee Chairman Alan Davidson, and in time became recognised as a man other greenkeepers could approach for advice in the knowledge that they would ‘get the right oil’.

Equipment in Grant’s tenure was basic to say the least. Essentially it consisted of a set of gang mowers towed behind the versatile Ferguson tractor, a set of Overgreen mowers behind which the operator would walk, and an Attco hand mower used to mow tight spots such as the edges of bunkers. An old model T Ford with a small tray served a great variety of transport requirements. This Model T had to be started with a crank handle though it was extremely reliable and generally started easily even on frosty mornings. When required Grant would bring his own Gravely tractor from his home property. The Gravely was a very versatile machine to which could be attached a number of implements including a rotary hoe, a sickle bar mower, and a rotary mower.

Later Grant developed a rotary broom to sweep cores into rows for easy removal from the greens. He also adapted an old Morrison lawn mower as a grooving machine for use on the greens.

In spite of this paucity of equipment and the fact that the Green Staff consisted of the Head Greenkeeper and just one assistant the course was kept in very good order throughout the year. Initially there were significant areas of deep rough which were mown with the sickle bar mower just two or three times a year.
