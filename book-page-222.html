the ball Don played a good second just short and to the right of the green. As we moved on the other fellow shouted that he had found the ball. He took a bit of time and a couple of shots to get it back to the fairway, then another couple to get within range of the green where we were waiting. What was perhaps his sixth shot sailed to the right where it struck Don’s bag as he waited to play his third. Under the rules of the game at that time Don had to concede that he had lost the hole. My father, as usual, let the incident be a lesson to me as he lectured me on the importance of keeping my gear and myself well out of the way of an opponent’s wayward stroke.

I did this religiously for some time though I still got caught out a couple of years later at Middlemore when an opponent shanked a short chip through a bunker onto my cart that was parked safely, I thought, beside the next tee.

The Club did not at first have a resident professional. Bronco Branch gave occasional lessons but was committed to his business in the town. Later Jim Paterson was appointed resident professional. I remember the first time Dad played in a four with Jim he was disappointed that Jim showed no apparent interest in anyone else’s golf. I don’t quite know what Dad expected. I know it must have been very hard going for Jim as paying for golf lessons would have seemed unimaginable extravagance to most members.

Jimmy Mowat had been the Professional before the Second World War and had regained his amateur status after it. He was a Scotsman of the old school who was strict on the rules of the game as well as those of the Club. Late in 1954 my father went on a teaching exchange to the USA, leaving his family home for the year. Before going he said he would pay my subscription for the year, six pounds, if I should make golf my chosen sport. As it was I was eager to get to College and play cricket and hockey so the subscription was not paid. Early the following year I had a round with a friend and his father, shooting that 81 referred to earlier.

That evening my mother got a ring from Jimmy to say that if I was not playing with my father I was not eligible to play on the course so that was the end of my golf for the year. Rules are rules I guess and the rule was that sons of members could play with their fathers, not with other members, but perhaps a bit of flexibility would have done no harm in the circumstances. Towards the end of the year Dad returned from America and soon after was appointed Headmaster at Clevedon School near Papakura so our time at the Tauranga Golf Club came to an end though I have enjoyed playing the course in tournaments and pennant competition on many a subsequent occasion.
