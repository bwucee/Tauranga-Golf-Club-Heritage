B category was restricted to 100 members and a further category C was introduced providing for midweek golf only. This category came to be seen as a waiting room for A category and at one stage that waiting time stretched to as much as five years. Sometimes a player would move from C to B as a stepping-stone to the A membership he really wanted. Ultimately priority was given to such B members to move up to full membership. Pressure for membership within the women’s section at no time reached the stage where a waiting list had to be instituted.

This sometimes caused problems when a wife would gain membership while her husband languished in frustration on the sidelines.

In December 1969 the membership committee reported the following figures: Men’s A - 337 (limit 335 + 5), Men’s B - 58 (limit 100), Men’s C - 50 (no limit), Ladies’ A - 282 (limit 300), Ladies’ B - 27 (limit 50). In addition there were 69 non-playing members, while 31 men and 21 women were listed as awaiting membership and 11 men sought a change of category. The reason 21 women were waiting upon membership was because they would accept membership only if and when their husbands gained admittance. At this time the priority for acceptance was to those seeking an upgrade followed by those holding National handicaps of four or better.

The limitation on membership continued through until 1985 when the committee were concerned over a down turn in membership figures following subscription renewals for the new season. As a result there were now vacancies for 23 male members. In response to this trend that was still apparent the following year the committee moved to increase the number of places available and to endeavour to attract new members. Category limits were consequently increased to allow up to 375 men in A category plus 25 discretionary places, up to 125 B category men, and up to 350 women in total over categories A and B, no more than 50 of those to be B category.

Shortly thereafter it was realised that category limits were no longer necessary and they have not been found to be necessary since. Quite the reverse has applied. Subsequent committees have actively campaigned to attract new members in order to ensure the financial viability of the Club. Two factors have had a strongly positive influence on maintaining membership levels. These have been the growth of Nine Hole Golf amongst women players, and the attraction of the game to the growing population of Asian Immigrants arriving in Tauranga.

Reg Ellis was about to tee off at the first when a rabbit shot across the tee with a weasel on its back. Reg made a swipe with his club, a much quicker swing than his trademark slow rhythmic golf swing, and killed them both. He then teed off and scored a three. Can anyone else claim to have scored a rabbit, a weasel, and a birdie at the same hole? Well perhaps not but Des Shiel can lay claim to the weasel. As Judge Ian Thomas was about to play his tee stroke on the first tee a few years ago another weasel ran across the tee. Ian had no idea what was going on as people shouted for someone to ‘get it’.

Several swung clubs at the weasel but, after chasing it out from a hole under the Moreton Bay tree, it was Des who proved himself the successful hunter.
