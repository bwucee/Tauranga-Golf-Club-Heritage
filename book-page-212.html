The spiking machine consisted of large, heavy, octagonal shaped steel wheels with ten-inch knives bolted to the corners of each wheel. This was dragged across the greens and the design was such that the knives penetrated deeply then ‘kicked’ back as they emerged. This was effective in breaking up the compaction that develops under the greens as a result of the pressure exerted by people and machinery passing across them.

The professional at that time was J A (Jimmy) Paterson. His business would be very slow over the summer and he would join us from time to time when we had extra work. Jimmy had a truly likeable personality and got on really well with Grant and I but he had virtually no confidence in using machinery of any kind. It was not wise to ask Jim to back the trailer down to the rubbish tip at the top of the gully below the implement shed unless you were prepared to haul the trailer back out of the pit later! Other than this occasional assistance from Jim green keeping was a two-man job for many years.

There is no doubt the course was less well cared for than it is today. We simply did not have the manpower, the machinery, or the financial resources to facilitate something better. None the less I am confident the membership got the very best that the circumstances of the time allowed, just as they do today. It is interesting to note that I was paid about £11 in 1957, the same as a senior subscription for a year so it required 50 members to cover my salary as the assistant green keeper. The same would be very nearly true today, the senior assistant’s salary being covered by some 50 members.

Of course there are more

assistants today so the cost of maintaining the course is relatively much higher.

There were many memorable moments in my time working on the course. Perhaps the most memorable involved a lesson in the unexpected dangers of smoking. In those pre-OSH days life was a little less regulated. When we needed to remove a row of trees along the left of the 14th fairway Grant came up with the novel idea that we would dynamite them out of the ground. He would use an auger to drill a hole down below the bole of the tree, place a stick of dynamite and a fuse then move on to the next tree. I would then light the fuse, retreat a safe distance to observe the result, then tow the tree away to the dump.

By the time I returned the next fuse would be ready to light. Everything worked beautifully for the first few trees, the timing was good and each tree came easily from the ground. Then, returning from the tip once again, I noticed Grant move away from the next tree in line so I bent down, matches in hand. Boom! The tree was blasted out of the ground inches in front of me before I could touch a match to the fuse. Grant, who was often prone to pause in his work to roll and light a cigarette, had done just that after setting the charge and had then automatically touched the match to the fuse before moving away.

Fortunately I was not hurt, only severely shaken. We were able to see the comic book humour of it a little later.

On Friday afternoons Noel Hockin, then Club Secretary, would come out from town with our pay packets and Noel, Grant, Arthur Towsey and I would spend a pleasant hour or two sitting on beer crates inside the little bar discussing all manner of things. This made for a very pleasant end to the working week. Those three people had a huge impact on the development of both the club and the course over a long period and I count it as a privilege to have had the opportunity to gain from their collective knowledge and understanding of so many aspects of club and course management.
