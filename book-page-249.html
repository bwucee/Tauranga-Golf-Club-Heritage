While Nagle was effectively eliminating himself from contention fellow Australian David Good scored 61 to match the record held by Nagle and Brask. Good’s ten birdies included twos at four of the five par threes, his one dropped shot coming from a five at the seventeenth. The course at the time these 61s were recorded measured 5602m, only 15m short of the course as measured from the blue tees today. Good led Brask by a stroke with another two strokes back to Lister commencing the third round and the three put even more distance on the rest of the field with the first pair scoring 65s and Lister a 67.

Good was the first to fade in the final round, his four bogeys in the middle of the round leading to a 72 and a third place finish earning him $1800 from the $20 000 prize pool, a very large purse at the time. Brask made the early move with a birdie at the second followed by an eagle and two more birdies in the next five holes. He looked all over the winner even when Lister started his own birdie barrage with four in succession starting with a two at the fifth. Following a spectacular shot from the now defunct fairway bunker to the heart of the 13th green Lister’s eagle putt lipped out, the birdie leaving him still three behind with only five to play.

To the disbelief of the gallery, and no doubt to his own, Brask bogeyed each of the final three holes to allow Lister to draw level at 17 under par. The stage was set for a dramatic play off made more pressured by the need for Brask to catch a plane for home. The playoff started at the 16th hole as only the latter part of the course was set up for TV coverage. The crowd that flocked there to follow the outcome was such that it enclosed the par four hole from tee to green two and three deep in places. The sight from the tee was impressive. The 16th was halved in pars before the pair moved on to the 17th where Lister launched an 8 iron from the centre of the fairway to within a metre of the pin to ensure the championship was his.

A memorable occasion in the history of competitive golf in Tauranga, both for the pleasure derived from a New Zealand victory, and for the play and deportment of the man who had set the standard from the first moments of the tournament, Bill Brask.

A number of Professionals from New Zealand and Australia were sponsored by Air New Zealand at that time to act as ambassadors to the company. As part of the deal they were required to assist in putting on clinics for interested juniors at tournaments such as this. John Lister was one such and his input to the clinic held in Tauranga was outstanding. His clear demonstrations and explanations were of exceptional value to those attending and anyone fortunate to be present during his explanation of the correct way to handle bunker shots would be unlikely to have had much problem with those since.

THE NZPGA CHAMPIONSHIP OF 1978

Scoring during the opening day that year was surely the poorest for any major tournament held on the course. Among the few to break par was the popular Anglo Australian Guy Wolstenholme, a great supporter of New Zealand tournaments over those years. Scores improved on the second day with Wolstenholme heading New Zealand amateur representative Geoff Clarke of St. Clair by a stroke with Bob Charles best of the rest on even par. In spite of tournament best cards of 64 from Simon Owen and Stuart Reese in the third round the tournament looked to be Wolstenholme’s for the taking following his second successive 67. This gave him a four-stroke lead at 7 under starting the final day.
