Nothing much had changed by the time Grant Scoones was appointed in 1999. The previous manager had been asked what he intended to do to prevent the Club running up deficits. His answer was that he would resign if the Club ran at a deficit the following year. There was a deficit, of course, though whether he honoured his pledge or was encouraged to move on is a moot point.

It has to be said that the Club environment was in a state of change at the time Grant Scoones came onto the scene. These changes were conducive to the Club moving forward in a manner that had not proved possible in the past. Central to this was the fact that the Board of Management was now firmly established, Board members understood their role and the Board was slowly gaining the confidence of a majority of the membership. The strength of the Board style of management lies in the restricted role the Board has. It is responsible for providing the best Course and Clubhouse facilities possible under prevailing circumstances. It must do this while ensuring that Club finances, staffing, and forward planning satisfy membership requirements as far as is reasonably possible.

The Board was not to be concerned with running competition golf, with promoting social activities, nor with the numerous, and often trivial matters, that committees of the past often found themselves involved with. Consequently Board members were expected to devote their energies to a concentrated sector of Club governance with the expectation that this would lead to better husbandry of financial resources. Similarly the General Manager was not contracted to involve himself in the organization of competitive golf. This was to remain the responsibility of the sectional committees. Inevitably there were areas related to competition where the Manager did become involved. These mainly related to major events such as the Five Course Classic and the Carrus Tournament, not to normal Club competition.

Another change related to the attitude of members. Who, even ten years before, would have believed that the Club would vote in General Meeting to raise women’s subscriptions to the same level as men’s? Further who would have believed that the Club would vote to allow the Board to raise subscriptions by up to 5% without reference to members? These changes were made in the first few years following the introduction of the Board and were symptomatic of changing attitudes. Where once sectional groups would lobby their members to attend AGMs and vote against progressive moves there was now wide recognition of the need to consider the greater good of all members, both present and future.

It may well have been thought that those attitudes that had previously stifled Club progress had now been

relegated to the past. In that regard judgement must remain suspended as subscription levels at the end of the first decade of the twenty-first Century remain at levels below those many administrators would consider necessary if the twin goals of covering current costs and building reserves against future capital needs are to be met. In order to facilitate its role the Board appoints a General Manager to implement the policies it decides upon. In addition the General Manager is expected to bring forward ideas for the Board’s consideration. Grant Scoones enjoyed the advantage of moving into a more responsive environment than did his predecessors. Equally importantly he was the right man for the time.
