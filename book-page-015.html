These were the aftermath of the European camp constructed during the Maori Wars. These trenches and some other fortifications, made beautiful by masses of white and blue flag lilies, made an unusual form of bunker. One tee was by Waimarie Guest House on McLean Street and the ‘fairway’ was an unformed road to a green by the gates of the Mission Chapel. The next hole was played from a high tee on the Northeast corner of the Domain extension down to an Island green surrounded by water at high tide - shades of modern USA course design perhaps.

This green was located where Odlin’s Timber Mill was later located. The land is now the site of the Trinity Hotel. Access to the green was by way of a few tussocky stepping- stones, particularly challenging to ladies wearing long skirts, as was the regulation golf attire of the day. The men were more appropriately turned out in knickerbockers. It was somewhat later that fashion conscious men turned to plus fours for added comfort and style.

The next hole extended eastwards along the flat towards the Military Cemetery. There were no roads, houses or railway, to impede progress, only a large frog pond in the middle of the fairway. The longest hole, ‘The Camp’, was some 420 yards and rated a bogey 6, bogey having the meaning that par has today. There were some unusual bogey ratings, the sixth and seventh holes being rated 3.5 and 4.5 respectively. Other than these two and the bogey-six second hole there was one bogey 5, four bogey

4s, and a single bogey 3, the final hole of only 105 yards. The bogey 5 eighth was only 210 yards so we have to presume it had to be negotiated in a double dog-leg pattern. The course of only 1860 yards (1690m) in length was rated bogey 38, a very generous rating by the standards of today. In spite of its lack of length the course must have represented a true challenge to the best of players considering the equipment available and the hazards encountered. After completing the first five holes the course returned along almost the same route it followed on the way out - reminiscent of the ‘R and A’ but perhaps not quite in the same class.

The final hole finished where it began at the ‘Clubhouse’, a tin shed shared by rugby players beneath huge pines on the Southern boundary of Wharepai Domain.

A card from that original Club was discovered in the archives held by the City Library and the names of holes recall some of the early history of the town. In order those names were Bowler, The Camp, End of the World, Dynamite, Railway, The Straight, The Cliff, Anzac, and Home. The origin of many of these names is obvious. Bowler confirms the bowling club was already in existence while Dynamite no doubt refers to the dangerous goods store that was on Sulphur Point. Anzac however is a mystery as this acronym supposedly originated from the Gallipoli campaign of World War 1. That surely came later than the date this card was printed.

In very early times Dominic Corrigan claimed he could drive a ball off his gold watch. When challenged he demonstrated this talent before a large and interested gallery on the first tee. The spectators were witness only to the total destruction of a very good watch.
