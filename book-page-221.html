Opening and Closing days were important as few people played over summer and there were no competitions over those months. I recall one closing day when a variety of competitions were held after the day’s play. There was a long driving contest where participants teed off from the top of the bank behind the 18th green. Dad was most surprised to be given a ball for being the only participant to land both his drives in the targeted area between the 11th and 18th fairways. There followed a short iron contest from somewhere near the ladies’ 7th tee to the 6th green. All those who made a two played again and I recall Dr. Mark winning with his second two.

He was a long handicapper and was absolutely delighted to have out-gunned all those lower handicapped players who had fancied their chances. Everyone then repaired to the clubhouse to enjoy High Tea followed by speeches and the presentation of the year’s major trophies.

For the Labour Weekend Tournament of 1953 I was allowed to earn myself some cash by caddying for someone other than my father so I was not privileged to witness the deed that highlighted my father’s golfing career. The details though are forever imprinted upon my mind. On the 6th, a par five of 507 yards, my father made an albatross two. The stand of Californian Redwoods on the hill between the 6th and 7th were then represented by a couple of newly planted saplings so, when Dad drove rather too much to the right past the large gum tree that once dominated the area, he found himself on a clear lie on a little flat terrace.

His way forward was blocked by the stand of pines beside the Ladies’ 7th tee but he had in his bag a four wood that he could rely on to produce a hook even when it was not desired. On this occasion he played up the 7th fairway and the club behaved as he wished it to, the ball drawing nicely around the trees and heading in the general direction of the green. The four did the usual searching and muttering until someone in desperation looked in the hole and there the ball nestled. Dad was not considered a long hitter and never before or since did he make the 6th green in two strokes.

To celebrate this previously unheard of feat the Club presented Dad with a pewter tankard that I have to this day.

In those earlier days Information about the wider world of golf was very sparse. The NZ Herald certainly covered New Zealand golf and British events such as the Open Championship but the United States PGA Tour was unknown to me. We had seen the film “Follow the Sun” starring Glen Ford as Ben Hogan and later I recall reading a detailed account of Hogan’s win in the Open at Carnoustie in 1953, the only time Hogan played in the Open Championship. He had been told he would not be considered one of the greats unless, or until, he added that title to his list of successes. Consequently Fred Ward’s reference to “The Masters” that day at the Gully had no meaning to me at the time.

Even golf played elsewhere in the Bay of Plenty was somewhat remote. I think the only local golfer to make the Bay teams at that time was Harry Dale from the Mount Club though Fred Ward and Jack Bateman had been members of Bay teams earlier. Ivan Deadman was one regular Bay representative who often played in tournaments at Tauranga. I also remember a young Don Boone playing in a four with Dad during a Labour Weekend tournament. That was at the beginning of Don’s long and illustrious career in Bay golf. I will never forget an incident I witnessed in Don’s first round match that year. On the 7th hole Don’s opponent hooked his tee shot into the gorse to the left while Don was long down the middle.
