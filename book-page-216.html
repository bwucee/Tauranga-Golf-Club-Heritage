The path to the green dipped down to swamp level before sloping upward to the green. Great was the delight when this path was built up a little to ease this climb back out of the gully. It has been further built up on at least two further occasions.

The 18th green was quite different in shape then. It was essentially square with a false front and a slope at the back running up towards the old clubhouse. The holes were all named and though I remember most of them to this day you never hear them identified this way any more. I feel that is really a pity. Sixty years ago most members referred to many of the holes by name rather than number. Today this applies to the Knoll, and perhaps the Gully and The Crescent. I wonder how many members today would recognise the 15th as Kaharoa. That hole was then played as a par five.

It was later reduced to a par 4 and by memory was then made the number one stroke hole. Dad always maintained the feature tree for which the 18th is named was not a Moreton Bay fig at all but I fear the Australians may disagree with this view.

The scorecards always carried the handicap allowances on the back. From memory you were allowed three quarters of your handicap in Par (then Bogey) and Stableford competition and five eighths of the difference in handicap match play. This later became three quarters in line with the other competitions. As 24 was the maximum handicap for male golfers no one ever got more than a stroke a hole in Stableford competition. The very idea of anyone getting two shots on a hole would really have upset the old timers. Strokes were owed on different holes in the two forms of play so the card featured two different handicap allocations. Handicaps were based on the two lowest rounds over the previous six months and it seemed handicaps hardly ever went outwards.

Playing to your handicap (36 points) was a real achievement and everyone congratulated you on the merit of such a performance.

On Race Days the course was essentially closed to allow for parking on the fairways and for safety reasons as many holes ran adjacent to areas where horses were stabled, loaded into floats, or saddled. I do remember Dad playing on one race day though. We were on the 13th when the horses thundered past. Clods of earth flew everywhere but even more impressive than that to a ten year old brought up very strictly by a father who did not swear was the sound of non-stop, very loud, cursing by the jockeys as they positioned themselves for the run home. Large wooden observation stands were sited on the fence around the crescent and these required a local rule to clarify the relief available.

I remember as a kid finding an earlier scorecard with different names such as Alpha and Omega for the 1st and 18th. There were other fascinating hints of earlier times. I recall someone telling a story of long ago before the Club owned a tractor. The tee boxes were filled with sand as the wooden golf tee had not then been invented and players teed the ball on a small pile of sand. The tee boxes were battery cases or similar that sat on the ground. Someone made a bet on the 14th tee that he could hit his ball from the top of the sand in the box and reach the green.

The result was one box virtually destroyed which the green keeper had to replace by wheeling a new one in his wheelbarrow all the way down to that very distant tee.
