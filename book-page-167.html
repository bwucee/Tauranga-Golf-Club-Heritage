Sadly, in 1958, he succumbed to Prostate Cancer at the early age of 63. With the treatment available today he would surely have enjoyed many more years of golf. As it was he fought on longer than expected and, despite being essentially bedridden for most of two years was able to shoot a 76 in his final round, this being at a very late stage of his illness. James Mowat, professional golfer, was ahead of his time in many ways.

How he would love to have contributed to, and participated in, the wave of popularity the game has enjoyed over more recent times.

JAMES A PATERSON

James A Paterson was appointed to the vacant Professional position in 1953 and served the Club in that role until 1972. Prior to his appointment members seeking professional instruction were accommodated by A J (Bronco) Branch who owned the Sports Store in Central Tauranga still known as Broncos. Bronco was never attached to the Club but was authorised to give lessons and provide equipment to any member requiring assistance.

Jim was born in England where his father was a Professional who had learned his trade in a region where Harry Vardon, one of the greatest players of all time, was located. Vardon had an enormous influence on the game throughout Britain and in the United States where his barnstorming tours in the company of fellow Jersey Islander Ted Ray were instrumental in introducing millions of Americans to the game in the early years of the Twentieth Century. The golfer of today may not recognise that when he or she locks the ‘pinkie’ of their top hand over the forefinger of their bottom hand in gripping the club they are adopting the ‘Vardon’ grip, introduced to the game by this great player and teacher and now used almost universally.

An older generation of players would have been told early in their golfing education that it was essential that they adopt the ‘Vardon’ grip. Harry Vardon was to influence the senior Paterson and inevitably much of Vardon’s method and philosophy of golf was transferred to the younger Paterson in his teaching. Few, if any, Professionals at that time could expect to make a living from tournament golf so when Jim joined the Professional ranks it was as an apprentice learning the trade. There was time to compete in some of the tournaments that were held and Jim had the thrill and the honour of winning the Dutch Open on one momentous occasion.

Another great English player with whom Jim had some association was Henry Cotton. Henry was the man who restored British golfing pride by winning the British Open for Britain after Americans had dominated the famous championship for many years. The popular Dunlop 65 golf ball that many older players will recall having played in earlier days was named for the 65 Henry posted in that famous victory. Cotton was resident in the same area of England and Jim met him in tournaments and on other occasions. One story Jim told was of playing in a tournament where no local rule had been posted to allow free relief for an embedded ball on the green or anywhere else for that matter, the embedded ball rule having not then been added to the Rule Book.

Henry Cotton, having requested and been denied relief for a ball so embedded on the second green, proceeded to play a wedge stroke resulting in a large divot being removed from the green. A local rule was promptly introduced and soon afterward the Rule Book was amended to allow relief for a ball embedded both on, and through, the green.
