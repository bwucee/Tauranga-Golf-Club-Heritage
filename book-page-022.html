More distant fairways

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 22 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

The obvious deficiencies of the Domain course and the increasing pressure of Rugby and other sports on the grounds would have encouraged members to seek an alternative location and in 1914 the decision was made to take up an available lease at the Racecourse Reserve. This was done in spite of the fact that the early portents for the land were far from encouraging. The area was described as bleak, treeless, and open to all the winds of heaven, especially the Westerlies that appear to have been more prevalent in those days. The President of the time termed it “the coldest place within 20 miles of Tauranga.”

The history of the Racecourse Reserve dates back to 1878 when it was Gazetted that the Governor of the then Colony of New Zealand had reserved from sale that parcel of land containing 200 acres known as the Racecourse Reserve, the land to be designated for ‘a recreation ground and racecourse’. An addition to The Act of 1880 provided that all sums of money received in respect of the lands were to be applied, after deducting costs and expenses, in improving the lands in respect of which the money was received. This provision has continued to apply throughout the history of the Reserve with the lessee clubs being the beneficiaries of the rentals they pay.

Initially Trustees appointed by, and responsible to, the Minister of Crown Lands administered the Reserve. These trustees were elected by the public every seven years and it was not unusual for a degree of lobbying to be undertaken prior to the election in order to ensure particular interests were catered to. In this regard the members of the Golf Club appear to have been somewhat slow to protect their interests. As a result the Racing Club had the right to park vehicles on the 8th, 9th, and 10th fairways each Race Day, and to allow traffic coming from the south to enter via the gate behind the 14th green and to then travel across the golf course to reach those parking areas.

This even happened on wet winter days with the expected damage to fairways. In those earlier times attendance at race meetings was much greater than is generally the case now and it was not unusual for all three fairways to be covered in cars. By the end of a race day the Golf Club fairways were usually littered with discarded tickets, lunch wrappings, chicken bones and the like, while on wet days they suffered badly as the soft ground became cut up by vehicles driving across them. Consequently relationships between the two neighbouring Clubs were often severely strained.

The situation improved during the Fifties when some effective lobbying by Golf Club representatives resulted in the election of Domain Board Trustees more sympathetic to the interests of golfers. Several years of negotiations eventually led to an amicable agreement being reached in 1961. Life Member and Honorary Solicitor J C (John) Hare was chiefly instrumental in achieving this agreement which covered a number of contentious issues. None the less, just one year later Secretary Noel Hockin wrote to the Racing Club expressing dismay that the Racing Club proposed breaking the agreement on parking and traffic movement for the scheduled Race Meeting to be held on the 14th of April 1962.

In spite of his objection 250 cars entered via the 13th fairway. On this occasion the steward in charge of parking cut the chain locking the gate behind the 14th green with bolt cutters. Even as late as June 1965, the Golf Club committee minutes record that it was still deemed necessary to secure that gate with chain and padlock should weather conditions be adverse. This was in spite of the fact that the right to park on the fairways had supposedly been rescinded in 1960.
