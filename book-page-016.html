The first tee

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 16 / 305Next page →
From the book: original views, recollections and terminology are retained. References to “today”, rules, staff or programmes describe the time of writing. Text extraction is being reviewed; use the page reference when suggesting a correction.

The local rules recorded on the card give some insight into the conditions faced by our pioneer players. They include:

1. Out of Bounds - Mr Rowe’s Property. Through the fence to the right of the course from the eighth tee. 2. In driving from the third tee should a ball fall into and remain in the water or sand adjoining the hole it may be lifted and dropped further away from the hole without penalty. 3. If a ball lands in the sea from the drive off at hole 3 the player may lift and drop the same an equal distance from the hole without penalty. 4. A ball lying in hoof marks or cattle tracks may be lifted and dropped behind without penalty. This does not apply to a ball in a hazard. 5.

All ditches on the course are hazards. 6. A ball lying on an intermediate putting green must be lifted and dropped off the green from a height of six inches without penalty at any point not more than two club lengths from the edge of the green, but not nearer to the hole to which it is being played. 7. A ball lying on or touching horse or cow dung may be lifted and dropped not more than a club length behind without penalty. If in a hazard the ball shall be dropped in the hazard. 8. Parties playing odd holes have no standing on the green. 9. On medal and match days parties not playing in the medal or match competition must give way to those who are playing. 10.

On Saturday afternoons gentlemen may pass ladies on any part of the course. 11. On Saturdays players shall not be permitted to play odd holes on Findlay’s paddock unless they have completed two rounds.

Another point of interest gleaned from this card is that the recognised competitions of the day were Medal, Match, and Bogey (Par). The ubiquitous Stableford system had yet to be invented by Dr Frank Stableford, an English gentleman searching for a way to overcome the problem of one bad hole ruining his round. It was at Wallasley Golf Club near Liverpool in 1931 that this plus handicapper first suggested the system be adopted and it spread rapidly around the golfing world, perhaps to the detriment of the more demanding formats. It is also interesting to note that the longest hole on the old Domain course was the number one/two stroke hole while the shortest was stroke seventeen/eighteen.

Jack Rowe, who lived in ‘Waimarie’ on the corner of Cameron Road and Brown Street, recalled that his father, Bert Rowe, somewhat reluctantly took up the game after much urging from his golfing friends.

After just six weeks of play he entered and won the championship and promptly gave the game away to return to bowls after declaring that golf was ‘just a kid’s game’. It would seem few others found the game, or perhaps the course and its unusual hazards, quite so easy. A report on the results of a mixed foursomes competition in 1904 record the winning pair of Mr. Sharp and Miss Bull as finishing five down on bogey (now par). Dr. Brown and Mrs. Corbett were eight down, while the remaining eight pairs finished either ten down or twelve down.

Initially no golf was played on Sundays in deference to the puritanical views of the Landlady of ‘The Elms’. When she sold all her sections Sunday golf became standard practice.
