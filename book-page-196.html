Noel’s wife Peg was always there to lend support and it was often Peg who did much of the prior work in buying a stock of prizes from which a selection could be placed on the table each week. Of the other members of the team Joan Brown kept track of the finances, Bruce (bow tie and all) and Marge Anderson checked the cards, Ian Dale, Peter Heeney, and Steve Stephens tended the barbecue, while John Robinson, David Mowat, Des and Noeline Shiel frequently served as bar tenders. Noeline and Des never left before the clubhouse, including the bar area, was clean and tidy ready for the weekend golf.

Mary Goodjohn was the caterer through most of those years and she co-operated fully in ensuring that there were ample side dishes to supplement the barbecued offerings. Later the barbecue was used on alternate weeks with Mary providing the full meal on other occasions. From small beginnings, when sixty participants would have been considered an excellent field, the popularity of twilight golf increased until fields of 100 or more were not unusual. The record attendance reached a staggering 158, rather more than could comfortably be accommodated. Since there was never any way of knowing how many might roll up between the scheduled starting period from 4.45 to 6pm the starter had to be very flexible in order to satisfy everyone.

To facilitate play for such large numbers four separate courses, each with its own printed card, were developed. Course A covered holes 1-10, while course B covered the same holes but started from the 7th. tee with players moving to the 1st. tee after completing the 10th. hole. Course C covered holes 3-12 while course D played

through 11- 18, then through holes one and two. These courses all start and finish in reasonable proximity to the clubhouse and special starting sheets were developed to co-ordinate the timing so that groups passing through common tees were not inconvenienced. These proved to work very well even under the requirements of large fields.

To ensure all contestants were fairly treated regardless of the holes they played three different 10 hole cards were developed. Had the regular 18 hole card been used this would not have been so as players

would have received different numbers of strokes depending upon the particular course they played. The new cards solved that problem. While the stroke allocation initially confused some players our card checkers, Bruce and Marge, were adept at explaining the system to them. The stroke allocation on these cards followed a different principle to that used on 18 hole cards where fairness in Match Play is the aim. With abbreviated rounds and Stableford scoring the aim was to allocate low strokes to holes where it was most difficult to score Stableford points, typically long par three and four holes. This was quite the reverse of the allocation appropriate to Match Play.

The heyday of Friday Evening Twilight Golf came in the Seventies and Eighties and, as with many other activities, circumstances changed and interest began to decline. Somewhat reluctantly Noel was persuaded to step aside from his involvement in order to see if others could rekindle interest. This does not appear to have been achieved despite the effort made. Perhaps it was just a case of the trend line turning inexorably downward. Has not the same thing happened with Indoor Cricket, the game of Pool, and the playing of “skinners” after golf on Saturdays?
