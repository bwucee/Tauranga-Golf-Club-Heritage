Handicapping through the years

David G. S. Mowat, editor · Published 2014

← Previous pageBook p. 200 / 305Next page →

Open original PDF at page 200 →

Until the advent of the modern handicapping system developed by the USGA in the sixties handicapping was in the hands of a handicapping committee that may very well have consisted of a single appointee. The committee had a poorly defined mode of operation and essentially used their best judgement as to when to cut and when to increase a player’s handicap, and by how much. These decisions were not made arbitrarily; it just seemed that way to a lot of players especially on those occasions when a cut was made. Men and women had quite different systems, the women having a much more stringent system under which their handicap was based on the best golf they played and one good round could place a player on a handicap to which they might well struggle to play for the next few months.

Reviews were done at six monthly intervals and if the one good round was not repeated the player might be let out a stroke or two. Consequently players often found themselves quite uncompetitive for long periods. In addition women were required to earn their first official handicap by playing a round in not more than 36 strokes over par and many a tear was shed when a promising round was destroyed by a single really bad hole. This often happened at holes such as the Gully and the Knoll that claim many victims other than novice women. At times those bunkers at the Gully must have seemed three metres deep and the sides of the Knoll virtually insurmountable.

Never the less the day would eventually arrive when that magic score of 108, or better, was achieved and the champagne could be uncorked. What a thrill and a relief it must have been!

Men on the other hand had it easy. Upon joining they were automatically placed on a 24 handicap and it was then up to them to learn to play to that, or better, in order to become competitive. It is possible that the memories of older members become confused as age creeps up on them but there are those who recall that virtually all members in those times eventually learned to play to their 24 handicap and usually improved upon it. In more modern times some seem satisfied to remain on more generous handicaps up to 36. (The slope system may result in men playing off course handicaps even higher than 36).

Perhaps the challenge once offered to the 24-handicapper to become competitive is no

longer there.

The handicapper would usually be generous to the novice and allow him a degree of success before “wielding the axe”. My own experience of the system came in my first season when, after a few months, I began to play with a mild degree of success whereupon I picked up the occasional remark about “burgling”. Very politely, as then befitted the approach of a junior to a senior member, I approached the handicapper, Mr Warwick Nicoll. “Excuse me Sir”, I said, “I am starting to get a bit of gentle ribbing regarding my form. Should you not be reviewing my handicap downward”? His response was to the effect that it would happen soon enough.

“You haven’t won enough just yet”. When the axe finally descended a few weeks later it did so with a vengeance, my handicap falling by some ten strokes over a period of two months or so. Both men and women were allowed only three quarters of their handicap when playing Stableford or Bogey (now called Par) competitions, and also three quarters of the difference in handicaps when playing matches. A score of 36 points would often be sufficient to win a Stableford competition and 40 points or more was very rare. Women would have considered 36 points exceptional.
